## Lecture 1: Introduction to Research

### Exam-Ready Notes

## 1. What is Research?

Research is a systematic process of identifying and understanding a
problem, reviewing existing knowledge, developing and testing an
approach, collecting and analysing data, and drawing conclusions. It
also involves understanding why and how the work succeeds or fails,
identifying limitations, and considering whether findings can be
generalized.

## 2. What Should a Researcher Do After Obtaining Findings?

1.  Explain where, why, and how the work succeeds or fails.
2.  Identify constraints and limitations.
3.  Examine whether findings can be generalized.
4.  Use diverse and extensive datasets where appropriate.
5.  Identify genuine future research directions.

## 3. Characteristics of Quality Research

Quality research should: - Build on and acknowledge previous work. - Be
reproducible. - Be generalizable within clearly stated limits. - Have a
logical rationale for its methods. - Be connected to relevant theories
or existing knowledge. - Report methods, data, and results honestly.

## 4. Bad Research

-   **Plagiarism:** using another person's work or ideas without proper
    acknowledgement.
-   **Self-plagiarism:** reusing one's own previously submitted or
    published work without appropriate disclosure.
-   **Data falsification:** changing, inventing, or fabricating data or
    results.
-   **Misrepresentation:** presenting information in a misleading way.
-   **Misleading participants:** deceiving participants in an unethical
    manner.

## 5. Five Basic Steps of Research

1.  Select and define the research problem.
2.  Review the relevant literature.
3.  Conduct experiments or collect evidence.
4.  Analyse, explain, and discuss the findings.
5.  Draw conclusions and write the research report.

## 6. Step 1: Select and Define the Research Problem

A researcher may receive an assigned topic or select one independently.
The problem should be clearly defined before starting.

Clarify the practical requirements: - Topic or research area. - Required
page or word count. - Presentation duration. - Acceptable source
types. - Submission deadline. - Available time and resources.

A well-defined problem helps determine what to investigate, what
evidence is needed, and whether the study is feasible.

## 7. Research Questions (RQs)

A research question is a clear question that states what a study aims to
investigate or answer.

### Characteristics of a Good RQ

-   **Clear:** understandable and unambiguous.
-   **Focused:** addresses a specific issue.
-   **Researchable:** can be answered using evidence.
-   **Feasible:** possible within available time and resources.
-   **Balanced in scope:** neither too easy nor too difficult or broad.
-   **Analytical where appropriate:** goes beyond a simple description
    when analysis is required.

### How to Develop RQs

1.  Identify the general topic.
2.  Identify the specific problem or issue.
3.  Determine important terms, variables, and dimensions.
4.  Consider what evidence could answer the question.
5.  Write a clear, focused, researchable question.
6.  Check feasibility against available constraints.

## 8. Keywords, Terms, Issues, and Variables

-   **Keywords:** important search terms used to find literature.
-   **Terms/concepts:** main ideas used in the study.
-   **Issues:** specific problems or concerns being investigated.
-   **Variables:** measurable characteristics that may change or differ.

## 9. Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or prediction about a phenomenon
or relationship between variables. It can be examined using evidence or
experiments. A hypothesis should be clear and testable.

### Research Question vs. Hypothesis

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Research Question                   Hypothesis
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Asks what the study investigates.   States a proposed prediction or
                                      explanation.

  Usually written as a question.      Usually written as a declarative
                                      statement.

  Guides the investigation.           Can be tested using collected
                                      evidence.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

## 10. Step 2: Literature Review

A literature review is an examination of relevant existing research,
publications, and credible sources related to a research topic.

### Purposes of a Literature Review

1.  Understand what is already known.
2.  Identify important theories, methods, and findings.
3.  Find research gaps or unanswered questions.
4.  Avoid unnecessary duplication.
5.  Refine the research problem and questions.
6.  Select suitable methods and relevant sources.

## 11. Research Problem and Research Gap

-   **Research problem:** the issue, difficulty, or question that the
    study aims to investigate.
-   **Research gap:** an area that existing research has not
    sufficiently addressed, explained, tested, or explored.

A genuine research gap can help justify why a new study is needed.

## 12. When a Topic Is Too Broad

A topic is too broad when it covers too many issues, populations,
locations, time periods, or dimensions to investigate properly.

Narrow it by specifying: - Location or study area. - Time period. -
Population or group. - Particular aspect or dimension. - Event or
phenomenon. - Specific task or outcome.

## 13. When a Topic Is Too Narrow

A topic may be too narrow when there is insufficient literature,
evidence, or data.

Ways to broaden it: 1. **Generalize:** move from a very specific case to
a broader concept. 2. **Consider currency:** include recent or relevant
developments. 3. **Change databases:** search additional academic
databases or collections. 4. **Use synonyms:** search alternative and
related terms. 5. **Explore related issues:** include closely connected
problems. 6. **Expand or remove dimensions:** reconsider restrictions
involving location, time, aspect, event, population, or person/group.

The aim is to find a scope that is neither too broad nor too narrow.

## 14. Step 3: Experiment and Data Collection

The researcher conducts experiments or collects evidence needed to
investigate the research questions or test the hypothesis. Data may come
from real-life settings or simulated/artificial environments, depending
on the research objective.

### Real-Life vs. Simulated/Artificial Data

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Real-life data                      Simulated/artificial data
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Collected from actual environments  Generated in a controlled or
  or events.                          artificial setting.

  May reflect real-world complexity.  Can help control conditions or
                                      create specific cases.

  May contain noise, missing values,  May not fully represent real-world
  and practical limitations.          conditions.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The choice should be justified by the research question and study
design.

## 15. Importance of Diverse and Extensive Datasets

A diverse dataset includes different cases or conditions relevant to the
research problem.

For example, a face-recognition dataset should ideally include different
poses, lighting conditions, backgrounds, image qualities, and noise
levels. Diversity helps evaluate whether a method works beyond a small
set of controlled examples. Dataset limitations should still be
reported.

## 16. Step 4: Analyse, Explain, and Discuss Findings

The researcher analyses the collected data and interprets the results.

The researcher should: 1. Analyse evidence using appropriate methods. 2.
Explain what the results mean. 3. Discuss whether findings answer the
research questions. 4. Explain where and why the approach succeeds or
fails. 5. Identify strengths, weaknesses, constraints, and limitations.
6. Consider whether findings can be generalized. 7. Identify possible
future research directions.

Results should be discussed honestly and not overstated.

## 17. Step 5: Conclusions and Research Writing

The conclusion summarizes the main findings and explains how the study
addresses its research problem or questions.

A good conclusion should: - Briefly restate the study's purpose. -
Summarize key findings. - Explain the main contribution. - Acknowledge
important limitations. - Mention reasonable future research directions.

## 18. Common Research Writing Formats

-   **Thesis or book:** an extensive document presenting research in
    detail.
-   **Journal/transactions manuscript:** a paper prepared for an
    academic journal or transactions publication.
-   **Conference/workshop proceedings paper:** a paper published in
    conference or workshop proceedings.
-   **Extended abstract:** a short but more detailed summary, often
    around two pages in the lecture material.
-   **Concise abstract:** a brief research summary; the lecture material
    gives approximately 15--200 words.

Exact length and structure depend on publication or institutional
requirements.

## 19. Citation and Bibliography

-   **Citation:** acknowledgement in the text identifying the source of
    an idea, statement, method, or finding.
-   **Bibliography/References:** the list of sources used or cited,
    formatted according to the required style.

Proper citation gives credit to original authors, supports claims, and
helps readers locate sources.

## 20. LaTeX

LaTeX is a document preparation system commonly used for technical and
academic writing. It is useful for formatting structured documents,
equations, tables, references, and research papers.

## 21. MiKTeX and TeXstudio

-   **MiKTeX:** a TeX/LaTeX distribution that provides tools and
    packages needed to compile LaTeX documents.
-   **TeXstudio:** an editor that helps users write and manage LaTeX
    source files.

They can be used together to prepare academic documents in LaTeX.

# Scenario-Based Exam Questions

### 1. Plagiarism

**Question:** A student copies paragraphs from a research paper without
citing the original author. What type of misconduct is this?

**Answer:** This is plagiarism because the student has used another
person's work without proper acknowledgement.

### 2. Self-Plagiarism

**Question:** A researcher submits the same previously published work as
a new paper without disclosure. What is the problem?

**Answer:** This is self-plagiarism because the researcher has reused
their own previous work without appropriate disclosure.

### 3. Data Falsification

**Question:** A researcher changes experimental results to make a method
appear more accurate. Is this acceptable?

**Answer:** No. This is data falsification or misrepresentation.
Research data and results must be reported honestly.

### 4. Broad Research Topic

**Question:** A student selects a topic covering many countries,
populations, and time periods. What should the student do?

**Answer:** The student should narrow the topic by specifying the
location, time period, population, research aspect, or outcome.

### 5. Narrow Research Topic

**Question:** A student cannot find enough literature on a very specific
topic. What can the student do?

**Answer:** The student can broaden the topic by using synonyms,
searching more databases, considering related issues, generalizing the
topic, or relaxing unnecessary restrictions.

### 6. Face Recognition Dataset

**Question:** A face-recognition model is trained only on clear,
front-facing images taken under good lighting. Why may it perform poorly
in real-world conditions?

**Answer:** The dataset lacks diversity. It does not represent
variations in pose, lighting, background, image quality, or noise. A
more diverse dataset is needed to evaluate real-world performance.

### 7. Reproducibility

**Question:** Why should a researcher clearly report the dataset,
methods, and experimental settings?

**Answer:** Clear reporting supports reproducibility by allowing other
researchers to understand and repeat the study.

### 8. Research Gap

**Question:** A literature review shows that existing studies have not
tested a method in a particular setting. What may this indicate?

**Answer:** It may indicate a research gap. The researcher should verify
that the gap is genuine and formulate a research problem and question
around it.

### 9. Feasibility

**Question:** A research question requires data and equipment that a
student cannot access before the deadline. What should the student do?

**Answer:** The student should revise the scope or research question so
that the study can be completed with the available time, data, and
resources.
