# Lecture 1: Introduction to Research

## Exam-Ready Notes

## 1. What is Research?

Research is a systematic process of identifying and understanding a problem, reviewing existing knowledge, developing and testing an approach, collecting and analysing data, and drawing conclusions.

It also involves understanding why and how the work succeeds or fails, identifying limitations, and considering whether findings can be generalized to other settings.

## 2. What Should a Researcher Do After Obtaining Findings?

After obtaining research findings, a researcher should:

1. Explain where, why, and how the work succeeds or fits.
2. Identify where, why, and how the approach fails.
3. Identify constraints and limitations.
4. Examine whether findings can be generalized to other settings.
5. Use diverse and extensive datasets or samples where appropriate.
6. Identify genuine future research directions.

## 3. Characteristics of Quality Research

Quality research should:

- **Build on previous work:** Acknowledge and use relevant existing research.
- **Be reproducible:** Other researchers should be able to repeat the research process.
- **Be generalizable:** Findings should apply to other relevant settings within clearly stated limits.
- **Have a logical rationale:** Research methods should have a reasonable justification.
- **Be connected to theory:** Research should be linked to relevant theories and existing knowledge.
- **Report honestly:** Methods, data, findings, and limitations should be presented accurately.

## 4. Bad Research

Bad research includes unethical or scientifically unreliable practices.

- **Plagiarism:** Using another person's work or ideas without proper acknowledgement.
- **Self-plagiarism:** Reusing one's own previously submitted or published work without appropriate disclosure.
- **Data falsification:** Changing, inventing, or fabricating data or results to support a particular claim.
- **Misrepresentation:** Presenting information in a misleading or dishonest way.
- **Misleading participants:** Deceiving research participants in an unethical manner.

## 5. Five Basic Steps of Research

| Step | Main activity |
|---|---|
| 1 | Select and define the research problem |
| 2 | Review relevant information and literature |
| 3 | Conduct experiments or collect data |
| 4 | Analyse, explain, and discuss findings |
| 5 | Draw conclusions and write the research report |

## 6. Step 1: Select and Define the Research Problem

Problem identification is the beginning of a research study. It is one of the most difficult and important steps.

A researcher must identify and clearly state the problem before starting the research.

A researcher may receive an assigned topic or select one independently.

### Determine the Research Requirements

Before selecting a topic or starting research, understand the practical requirements:

- What problem are you trying to solve?
- Have you been assigned a topic, or can you choose your own?
- How many pages or words are required?
- How long should the presentation be?
- Are specific types of sources required, such as journals or books?
- What is the submission deadline?
- How much time and resources are available?

A well-defined problem helps determine what to investigate, what evidence is needed, and whether the study is feasible.

## 7. Research Questions (RQs)

A research question is a clear question that states the specific issue or problem a research study aims to investigate.

It guides the direction and scope of the research.

### Characteristics of a Good Research Question

There is no universal set of criteria for a good research question. However, a good RQ should generally be:

- **Clear:** Understandable and unambiguous.
- **Focused:** Addresses a specific issue.
- **Researchable:** Can be answered using available evidence.
- **Feasible:** Can be investigated within available time and resources.
- **Not too easy:** Should require meaningful investigation rather than a simple yes/no answer.
- **Not too difficult:** Should be answerable with accessible data and suitable methods.
- **Analytical:** Should examine relationships, reasons, effects, or explanations rather than only describe facts.

### How to Develop Research Questions

1. Identify the general research topic.
2. Write down what is already known about the topic.
3. Identify what is not known or not sufficiently understood.
4. Identify the specific problem or issue.
5. Select important keywords, terms, issues, and variables.
6. Consider possible relationships between the variables.
7. Identify what evidence could answer the questions.
8. Write clear, focused, and researchable questions.
9. Check whether the questions are feasible within available constraints.

Use probing questions such as **Why?**, **How?**, and **What if?** to develop analytical research questions.

### Example: Endangered Species Act (ESA)

The Endangered Species Act (ESA) is a law that protects animals and habitats that are in danger of extinction.

A researcher can begin by identifying what they already know and what they do not know.

**What I know:**

- The ESA protects animals and habitats that are in danger of extinction.
- Bald eagles and grizzly bears were once included on the endangered species list.
- There has been controversy about adding polar bears to the list.

**What I do not know:**

- What conditions are required for an animal to be removed from the endangered species list?
- What penalties are imposed for violating the Act?
- Does the Act protect only habitats within the United States?
- Does it cover ecosystems as well as individual animals?

**Possible Research Questions:**

1. What was the ESA designed to protect: animals only or ecosystems as well?
2. What animals or habitats outside the United States are covered by the Act?
3. What other countries have legislation to protect animals and habitats?
4. What animals are currently on the endangered species list?
5. How does an animal get added to or removed from the endangered species list?
6. What penalties are imposed on those who violate the Act?

This example demonstrates how existing knowledge and knowledge gaps can help develop research questions.

## 8. Keywords, Terms, Issues, and Variables

Before conducting a literature review, identify the important concepts related to the research topic.

- **Keywords:** Important search terms used to find relevant literature.
- **Terms/Concepts:** Main ideas or concepts used in the research.
- **Issues:** Specific problems or concerns being investigated.
- **Variables:** Measurable characteristics that may change or differ.

Identifying these elements helps organize the research and find relevant literature.

## 9. Hypothesis and Proposed Solution

A hypothesis is an idea or explanation based on known facts or reasonable assumptions that has not yet been specifically tested.

In other words, it is a proposed explanation or prediction about a phenomenon or relationship between variables that can be examined using evidence or experiments.

A hypothesis should be clear and testable.

Alternatively, a researcher may propose a possible solution to the identified problem.

### Research Question vs. Hypothesis

| Research Question | Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Asks what the study investigates. | States a proposed prediction or explanation. |
| Usually written as a question. | Usually written as a declarative statement. |
| Guides the investigation. | Can be tested using collected evidence. |
| Identifies what needs to be answered. | Suggests an expected outcome or relationship. |

**Example:**

Research Question: Does increasing study time improve students' academic performance?

Hypothesis: Increasing study time positively affects students' academic performance.

## 10. Step 2: Reviewing Information and Background Research (Literature Review)

A literature review is an examination of existing research, publications, and credible sources related to a research topic.

It helps researchers understand existing knowledge before conducting their own study.

### Purposes of a Literature Review

1. Provides a good overview of a topic, especially when the researcher is unfamiliar with it.
2. Helps identify important facts, terminology, dates, events, history, and organizations.
3. Identifies existing theories, methods, and research findings.
4. Helps identify research gaps and unanswered questions.
5. Helps avoid unnecessary duplication of previous work.
6. Leads to bibliographies that provide additional sources of information.
7. Helps re-examine the purpose of the study.
8. Helps redefine the research problem more clearly.
9. Helps determine an appropriate research methodology.

**Note:** The nature and scope of a literature review can vary from one research domain to another.

### Planning During Literature Review

A researcher may prepare alternative plans such as **Plan A, Plan B, and Plan C**.

However, alternative plans should remain connected to the main research objective. A researcher should not move too far away from the original research direction without a valid reason.

After reviewing the literature, the researcher can refine the problem and decide on a suitable research methodology.

## 11. Research Problem and Research Gap

- **Research Problem:** The issue, difficulty, or question that a study aims to investigate.
- **Research Gap:** An area that existing research has not sufficiently addressed, explained, tested, or explored.

A genuine research gap helps justify why a new study is necessary.

## 12. Refining the Research Topic: Too Broad or Too Narrow

A research topic should have an appropriate scope. It should be neither too broad nor too narrow.

### A. When a Topic Is Too Broad

A topic is too broad when it covers too many issues, populations, locations, time periods, or dimensions to investigate properly.

If a researcher finds too much information, the topic may need to be narrowed.

**Example:**

- Broad Topic: Global Warming
- Narrower Topic: How will climate change impact sea levels and coastal Bangladesh?

A topic can be narrowed by specifying:

- Location or study area.
- Time period.
- Population or group.
- Particular aspect or dimension.
- Event or phenomenon.
- Specific task or outcome.

### B. When a Topic Is Too Narrow

A topic may be too narrow when there is insufficient literature, evidence, or data available.

It may also be too specialized or too current to have enough published research.

**Example:**

- Narrow Topic: Does cartoon viewing cause aggression in children under five?
- Broader Topic: What are the negative effects of television on children and adolescents?

### Strategies to Broaden a Narrow Topic

| Strategy | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Generalize | Move from a very specific case to a broader concept. |
| Currency | If the topic is too recent, consider a less recent but related topic with more available literature. |
| Database Choice | Search other databases in the same or related subject areas. |
| Synonyms | Use alternative words and related search terms. |
| Related Issues | Explore closely connected problems or topics. |
| Expand/Remove Dimensions | Reconsider restrictions involving location, time period, aspect, event, population, or person/group. |

**Important:** The aim is to find a research scope that is manageable and has sufficient available evidence.

## 13. Step 3: Experiment and Data Collection

In this step, the researcher conducts experiments or collects/produces data needed to investigate the research questions or test the hypothesis.

Data may be collected through:

- Experiments.
- Surveys.
- Real-life observations or measurements.
- Existing datasets.
- Simulated or artificial environments.

The choice depends on the research objective and availability of data.

### Real-Life Data vs. Simulated/Artificial Data

| Real-life Data | Simulated/Artificial Data |
|---|---|
| Collected from actual environments or events. | Generated in a controlled or artificial setting. |
| May reflect real-world complexity. | Can help control conditions or create specific cases. |
| May contain noise, missing values, and practical limitations. | May not fully represent real-world conditions. |
| Useful for evaluating real-world performance. | Useful when real-life data is difficult to obtain or when controlled experiments are needed. |

The researcher should justify the choice based on the research question and study design.

### Importance of Diverse and Extensive Datasets

A diverse dataset contains different cases, conditions, or variations relevant to the research problem.

**Example: Face Recognition**

A face-recognition dataset should ideally include:

- Front-facing faces.
- Inclined or tilted faces.
- Different lighting conditions (less and more light).
- Clear images.
- Noisy or low-quality images.
- Different backgrounds and conditions.

If a model is trained only on clear, front-facing images under good lighting, it may perform poorly in real-world situations.

Therefore, diverse and extensive datasets help evaluate whether a research method works beyond a small set of controlled examples.

## 14. Step 4: Analyse, Explain, Interpret, and Discuss Findings

After collecting data, the researcher analyses and interprets the evidence.

The researcher should:

1. Analyse the collected data using appropriate methods.
2. Explain and interpret what the results mean.
3. Discuss whether the findings answer the research questions.
4. Explain where, why, and how the approach succeeds.
5. Explain where, why, and how the approach fails.
6. Identify the advantages and disadvantages (pros and cons).
7. Identify strengths, weaknesses, constraints, and limitations.
8. Examine whether findings can be generalized.
9. Identify genuine future research directions.

Results should be discussed honestly and should not be overstated.

## 15. Step 5: Conclusions and Research Writing

After analysing and discussing the findings, the researcher should draw proper conclusions and prepare the research report.

### Characteristics of a Good Conclusion

A good conclusion should:

- Briefly restate the research purpose.
- Summarize the main findings.
- Explain how the study addresses the research problem or questions.
- Highlight the main contribution.
- Acknowledge important limitations.
- Mention reasonable future research directions.

### Common Research Writing Formats

| Format | Description |
|---|---|
| Thesis or Book | An extensive document presenting research in detail. |
| Journal/Transactions Manuscript or Paper | A research paper prepared for an academic journal or transactions publication. |
| Conference/Workshop Proceedings Paper | A research paper published in conference or workshop proceedings. |
| Extended Abstract | A short but relatively detailed summary of the research; approximately two pages in the lecture material. |
| Concise Abstract | A brief summary of the research; approximately 15–200 words in the lecture material. |

Exact length and structure depend on the publication or institutional requirements.

## 16. Citation and Bibliography

- **Citation:** An acknowledgement in the research text identifying the source of an idea, statement, method, or finding.
- **Bibliography/References:** A list of sources used or cited in a research document, formatted according to the required referencing style.

Proper citation:

1. Gives credit to original authors.
2. Avoids plagiarism.
3. Supports research claims.
4. Helps readers locate original sources.

## 17. LaTeX and Research Writing

LaTeX is a document preparation system commonly used for technical and academic writing.

It is useful for preparing structured academic documents containing:

- Mathematical equations.
- Tables.
- Figures.
- References and citations.
- Research papers and theses.

### MiKTeX

MiKTeX is a TeX/LaTeX distribution that provides tools and packages required to compile LaTeX documents.

### TeXstudio

TeXstudio is an editor that helps users write, edit, and manage LaTeX source files.

### Using MiKTeX and TeXstudio

They can be used together to prepare academic documents:

1. Install a LaTeX distribution such as MiKTeX.
2. Install an editor such as TeXstudio.
3. Write the research document using LaTeX.
4. Compile the LaTeX source.
5. Convert or generate the final document in PDF format.

**Final output:** Research reports are commonly submitted in PDF format.

# Scenario-Based Exam Questions

## 1. Plagiarism

**Question:** A student copies paragraphs from a research paper without citing the original author. What type of misconduct is this?

**Answer:** This is plagiarism because the student has used another person's work without proper acknowledgement.

## 2. Self-Plagiarism

**Question:** A researcher submits the same previously published work as a new paper without disclosure. What is the problem?

**Answer:** This is self-plagiarism because the researcher has reused their own previous work without appropriate disclosure.

## 3. Data Falsification

**Question:** A researcher changes experimental results to make a method appear more accurate. Is this acceptable?

**Answer:** No. This is data falsification or misrepresentation. Research data and results must be reported honestly.

## 4. Broad Research Topic

**Question:** A student selects a topic covering many countries, populations, and time periods. What should the student do?

**Answer:** The student should narrow the topic by specifying the location, time period, population, research aspect, or outcome.

## 5. Narrow Research Topic

**Question:** A student cannot find enough literature on a very specific topic. What can the student do?

**Answer:** The student can broaden the topic by using synonyms, searching more databases, considering related issues, generalizing the topic, or relaxing unnecessary restrictions.

## 6. Face Recognition Dataset

**Question:** A face-recognition model is trained only on clear, front-facing images taken under good lighting. Why may it perform poorly in real-world conditions?

**Answer:** The dataset lacks diversity. It does not represent variations in pose, lighting, background, image quality, or noise. A more diverse dataset is needed to evaluate real-world performance.

## 7. Reproducibility

**Question:** Why should a researcher clearly report the dataset, methods, and experimental settings?

**Answer:** Clear reporting supports reproducibility by allowing other researchers to understand and repeat the study.

## 8. Research Gap

**Question:** A literature review shows that existing studies have not tested a method in a particular setting. What may this indicate?

**Answer:** It may indicate a research gap. The researcher should verify that the gap is genuine and formulate a research problem and question around it.

## 9. Feasibility

**Question:** A research question requires data and equipment that a student cannot access before the deadline. What should the student do?

**Answer:** The student should revise the scope or research question so that the study can be completed with the available time, data, and resources.

## 10. Developing Research Questions

**Question:** A student wants to study the Endangered Species Act but does not know which aspects to investigate. What should the student do?

**Answer:** The student should identify what is already known and unknown about the topic, select important keywords and issues, and develop focused research questions from the knowledge gaps.

## 11. Literature Review and Methodology

**Question:** Why should a researcher conduct a literature review before deciding on the research methodology?

**Answer:** A literature review helps the researcher understand existing methods, identify research gaps, refine the problem, and select an appropriate methodology.

## 12. Alternative Research Plans

**Question:** A researcher faces difficulties with the original research plan. What should the researcher do?

**Answer:** The researcher may consider alternative plans such as Plan A, Plan B, or Plan C, but should remain connected to the main research objective.

## 13. Real-Life and Simulated Data

**Question:** Real-life data is difficult to obtain for an experiment. What alternative can a researcher consider?

**Answer:** The researcher can use simulated or artificial data if it is suitable for the research objective and the limitations are clearly acknowledged.

## 14. Topic Is Too Current

**Question:** A researcher finds very few books or journal articles because the selected topic is extremely recent. What can the researcher do?

**Answer:** The researcher can consider an alternative or related topic that is less recent and has more available literature, or use additional databases and relevant sources.

# Broad Questions and Exam-Ready Answers

## 1. Explain the Five Basic Steps of Research.

**Answer:**

Research generally consists of five basic steps:

1. **Select and Define the Problem:** Identify and clearly state the research problem. Determine the requirements, scope, time, and available resources.
2. **Review the Literature:** Examine existing research to understand the topic, identify gaps, and refine the research questions.
3. **Conduct Experiments or Collect Data:** Collect or produce relevant evidence through experiments, surveys, real-life data, or simulated data.
4. **Analyse, Explain, and Discuss Findings:** Analyse the evidence, interpret results, discuss strengths and weaknesses, and identify limitations and future scopes.
5. **Draw Conclusions and Write the Report:** Summarize the findings, explain the contribution, acknowledge limitations, and prepare the research report.

## 2. Explain the Characteristics of Quality Research.

**Answer:**

Quality research should have the following characteristics:

- It should be based on and acknowledge previous research.
- It should be reproducible by other researchers.
- Its findings should be generalizable to relevant settings within justified limits.
- It should follow a logical rationale.
- It should be connected to relevant theories and existing knowledge.
- It should report methods, data, and results honestly.

These characteristics improve the reliability, credibility, and scientific value of research.

## 3. What Is a Good Research Question? Explain Its Characteristics.

**Answer:**

A research question is a clear question that identifies the specific issue a research study aims to investigate.

A good research question should be clear, focused, researchable, and feasible. It should not be too easy or too difficult to answer. It should encourage meaningful investigation and preferably be analytical rather than merely descriptive.

The researcher should identify what is already known and unknown, select relevant keywords and variables, and determine what evidence can answer the question.

## 4. Explain the Importance of a Literature Review.

**Answer:**

A literature review examines existing research and publications related to a research topic.

Its importance includes:

1. Providing an overview of the topic.
2. Identifying important facts, terminology, history, and concepts.
3. Understanding existing theories, methods, and findings.
4. Identifying research gaps.
5. Avoiding unnecessary duplication.
6. Providing additional sources through bibliographies.
7. Refining the research problem and purpose.
8. Helping select a suitable research methodology.

Therefore, a literature review provides the foundation for a well-planned research study.

## 5. How Can a Researcher Refine a Broad or Narrow Topic?

**Answer:**

A research topic should be neither too broad nor too narrow.

If a topic is too broad, the researcher should narrow it by specifying the location, time period, population, aspect, event, or outcome.

If a topic is too narrow, the researcher can broaden it by generalizing the topic, considering less recent developments, searching additional databases, using synonyms, exploring related issues, or removing unnecessary restrictions.

The objective is to develop a focused and feasible research topic with sufficient available literature and evidence.

## 6. Explain the Importance of Diverse and Extensive Datasets.

**Answer:**

Diverse and extensive datasets are important because they help evaluate whether a research method performs well under different conditions.

For example, a face-recognition dataset should include different face poses, lighting conditions, backgrounds, image qualities, and noise levels.

A dataset containing only clear, front-facing images may not represent real-world situations. Therefore, dataset diversity improves the evaluation of model performance and helps identify limitations in generalization.

## 7. Differentiate Between Real-Life and Simulated Data.

**Answer:**

Real-life data is collected from actual environments or events, while simulated data is generated in a controlled or artificial setting.

Real-life data may reflect real-world complexity but can contain noise, missing values, and practical limitations. Simulated data allows researchers to control conditions and create specific cases, but it may not fully represent real-world situations.

The choice depends on the research question, objectives, and data availability.

## 8. What Should a Researcher Do During Analysis and Discussion?

**Answer:**

During analysis and discussion, a researcher should analyse the collected evidence using suitable methods and interpret the results.

The researcher should explain where and why the approach succeeds or fails, discuss its advantages and disadvantages, identify constraints and limitations, examine whether the findings can be generalized, and suggest genuine future research directions.

## 9. Explain Common Research Writing Formats.

**Answer:**

Research findings can be presented in several formats:

- **Thesis or Book:** A detailed document presenting the research.
- **Journal/Transactions Paper:** A manuscript prepared for academic journal publication.
- **Conference/Workshop Proceedings Paper:** A paper published in conference or workshop proceedings.
- **Extended Abstract:** A relatively detailed short summary, often around two pages.
- **Concise Abstract:** A brief summary, approximately 15–200 words according to the lecture material.

All research reports should follow appropriate citation and bibliography requirements.
