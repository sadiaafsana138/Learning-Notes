# RESEARCH QUESTIONS, HYPOTHESES, AND STUDY DESIGN

## 1. Introduction to Research Questions and Study Design

Research begins with identifying a problem and understanding its nature. After identifying the problem, researchers review existing literature, identify research gaps, formulate research questions, develop hypotheses where appropriate, and design a suitable study.

A research study generally follows this process:

1. Identify a research problem.
2. Review relevant literature.
3. Identify a research gap.
4. Formulate research questions (RQs).
5. Develop hypotheses, if applicable.
6. Define constructs and variables.
7. Select an appropriate research design.
8. Identify data sources and collection methods.
9. Analyze the collected evidence.
10. Interpret results and draw conclusions.

**Main objective:** To develop a logical, evidence-based, feasible, and ethically defensible research plan.

---

## 2. Problem Statement

### Definition

A problem statement is a clear and concise description of a research problem that explains what is wrong, what is unknown, why it matters, and what needs to be investigated.

It establishes the foundation of a research study.

### Components of a Good Problem Statement

A strong problem statement generally includes:

1. **Context:** Introduces the research area and background.
2. **Specific Problem:** Identifies the issue that needs investigation.
3. **Existing Evidence:** Explains what previous studies have established.
4. **Research Gap:** Identifies what remains unknown or insufficiently studied.
5. **Research Direction:** Indicates what the proposed study intends to investigate.

### Problem Statement Formula

**Context + Specific Problem + Existing Evidence + Research Gap + Research Direction**

### Example

Weak statement:

"Machine learning is important for agriculture, and crop yield prediction is useful."

Strong statement:

"Machine learning has been increasingly applied to agricultural yield prediction. However, models trained using data from selected geographical regions may not perform consistently in geographically unseen districts because of differences in climatic and agroecological conditions. Therefore, this study investigates the spatial transferability of machine learning-based crop yield prediction models across different districts."

### Characteristics of a Good Problem Statement

- Clear and specific.
- Researchable using available evidence.
- Supported by existing literature.
- Identifies a meaningful gap.
- Relevant to the research domain.
- Feasible within available time and resources.

---

## 3. Research Gap

### Definition

A research gap is an unanswered question, limitation, or insufficiently explored area in existing research that provides an opportunity for further investigation.

A research gap explains why a new study is necessary.

### Common Types of Research Gaps

| Type | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge Gap | Important knowledge is missing | Limited understanding of a phenomenon |
| Methodological Gap | Existing methods have limitations | Only random train-test splitting is used |
| Contextual Gap | A topic has not been sufficiently studied in a specific context | Limited research in Bangladesh |
| Population Gap | A population is underrepresented | Rural communities are excluded |
| Temporal Gap | Existing research does not cover recent changes | Studies use outdated datasets |
| Theoretical Gap | Existing theories do not sufficiently explain a phenomenon | Existing theory cannot explain observed behavior |
| Empirical Gap | Insufficient evidence exists | Few empirical evaluations are available |

### How to Identify a Research Gap

1. Search for relevant research papers.
2. Review their research objectives and methods.
3. Examine datasets and study populations.
4. Identify limitations mentioned by authors.
5. Compare findings across studies.
6. Identify unanswered questions.
7. Verify whether the proposed gap is genuinely meaningful.

**Important:** A research gap should be supported by literature, not simply by claiming that nobody has studied the topic.

---

## 4. Research Question (RQ)

### Definition

A research question is a clear, focused, and researchable question that defines what a study intends to investigate.

It guides the research design, data collection, analysis, and interpretation.

### Characteristics of a Good Research Question

A good research question should be:

1. **Clear:** Easy to understand.
2. **Focused:** Addresses a specific research problem.
3. **Researchable:** Can be answered using evidence.
4. **Feasible:** Can be investigated with available resources.
5. **Relevant:** Contributes to existing knowledge.
6. **Ethical:** Can be investigated without unacceptable ethical risks.
7. **Aligned:** Consistent with the problem statement and research objectives.

### Relationship Between Research Gap and RQ

Research Gap → Research Question → Evidence → Method → Analysis → Contribution

A research question should emerge logically from an identified research gap.

### Anatomy of a Research Question

A well-developed RQ may contain:

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Population / Context | Who or what is being studied |
| Phenomenon | What is being investigated |
| Constructs / Variables | What factors or concepts are involved |
| Evidence | What information is needed |
| Contribution | What the study aims to explain, compare, or improve |

### Weak vs. Strong Research Questions

| Weak RQ | Strong RQ |
|---|---|
| Is machine learning useful? | How accurately can machine learning models predict crop yield using historical climate variables? |
| Is social media harmful? | How does exposure to misleading social media content influence users' information-verification behavior? |
| Can AI improve education? | To what extent does an AI-assisted feedback system improve students' writing performance compared with conventional feedback? |

### Why Is the Strong Version Better?

It specifies the research context, variables or phenomenon, and the type of evidence required.

## 5. Types of Research Questions

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Describes a phenomenon | What are the common patterns of crop yield across districts? |
| Comparative | Compares groups or methods | Does Model A perform better than Model B? |
| Relational | Examines relationships between variables | Is rainfall associated with crop yield? |
| Causal | Investigates cause-and-effect relationships | Does a specific intervention improve learning outcomes? |
| Interpretive | Understands experiences or meanings | How do farmers perceive climate-related risks? |
| Design / Evaluation | Develops or evaluates a system | How usable is a proposed agricultural information system? |

**Important distinction:** A relationship between two variables does not automatically establish causation.

### Research Question Revision Checklist

Before finalizing an RQ, ask:

- Is the question clear?
- Is it sufficiently focused?
- Does it address the research gap?
- Can it be answered using available evidence?
- Are the required variables or constructs identifiable?
- Is the method suitable?
- Is the scope realistic?
- Is the question ethically acceptable?

## 6. Research Objectives

### Definition

Research objectives are specific statements describing what a study intends to achieve.

They translate the research problem and research questions into actionable goals.

### Types of Objectives

**General Objective:** Describes the overall purpose of the study.

**Specific Objectives:** Describe individual tasks or outcomes required to achieve the general objective.

### Example

General Objective:

"To investigate the spatial transferability of machine learning-based crop yield prediction models."

Specific Objectives:

1. To evaluate the effect of climate and agroecological variables on prediction performance.
2. To compare crop-specific seasonal modelling approaches.
3. To assess model generalization across geographically unseen districts.
4. To investigate how climatic and geographical similarity influences prediction errors.

### Relationship

Problem Statement → Research Objectives → Research Questions → Methods → Expected Outcomes

Objectives should be consistent with the research questions.

## 7. Hypothesis

### Definition

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement predicting a relationship, difference, or effect between variables.

It is developed based on existing theories, previous findings, and logical reasoning.

### Types of Hypotheses

#### A. Null Hypothesis (H0)

The null hypothesis states that there is no relationship, difference, or effect in the population being studied.

Example:

H0: There is no significant difference in prediction performance between Model A and Model B.

More precisely, the null hypothesis specifies the absence of the relevant population effect or difference.

#### B. Alternative Hypothesis (H1 or Ha)

The alternative hypothesis states that a relationship, difference, or effect exists.

Example:

H1: Model A has significantly lower prediction error than Model B.

### Directional and Non-Directional Hypotheses

| Type | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Directional | Predicts the direction of a relationship or difference | Model A will have lower RMSE than Model B |
| Non-directional | Predicts a difference without specifying direction | Model A and Model B will have different RMSE values |

### When Should a Hypothesis Be Used?

Hypotheses are appropriate when:

- The study tests relationships between variables.
- The study compares groups or methods.
- The study evaluates an intervention.
- The study uses statistical testing.

### When May a Hypothesis Not Be Necessary?

A formal hypothesis may not be necessary for:

- Exploratory research.
- Descriptive research.
- Qualitative interpretive research.
- Early-stage design research without a testable prediction.

**Important:** Not every research question requires a hypothesis.

## 8. Independent and Dependent Variables

### Independent Variable (IV)

An independent variable is a variable that is considered a predictor, explanatory factor, or potential cause in a study.

### Dependent Variable (DV)

A dependent variable is the outcome or response variable that the study aims to explain or predict.

### Example

Research question:

"How does rainfall influence crop yield?"

- IV: Rainfall.
- DV: Crop yield.

### Multiple Variables Example

For crop yield prediction:

| Variable | Role |
|---|---|
| Rainfall | Independent / Predictor |
| Temperature | Independent / Predictor |
| Humidity | Independent / Predictor |
| Agroecological zone | Independent / Predictor |
| Crop yield | Dependent / Target |

### Direction of Relationship

A hypothesis may predict:

- Positive relationship.
- Negative relationship.
- No relationship.
- Difference between groups.

**Important:** In predictive machine learning, predictor and target variables are often used instead of causal IV and DV terminology. Predictive association does not prove causation.

## 9. Construct, Variable, Dimension, Indicator, and Measure

These concepts help researchers convert abstract ideas into measurable evidence.

### Construct

A construct is an abstract concept that cannot always be directly observed.

Examples:
- Trust.
- Satisfaction.
- Usability.
- Motivation.
- Climate vulnerability.

### Variable

A variable is an attribute or characteristic that can take different values.

Examples:
- Age.
- Temperature.
- Income.
- Test score.

### Dimension

A dimension is a specific aspect of a broader construct.

Example:

Usability may include:
- Learnability.
- Efficiency.
- Satisfaction.
- Error recovery.

### Indicator

An indicator is an observable sign used to represent a construct or dimension.

Example:

Number of errors during a usability task.

### Measure

A measure is the specific method or instrument used to quantify an indicator.

Example:

Error count recorded during usability testing.

### Operational Definition

An operational definition explains exactly how a construct or variable will be measured in a study.

Example:

"Usability will be measured using the System Usability Scale (SUS), a standardized questionnaire."

### Relationship

Construct → Dimension → Indicator → Measure → Data

| Concept | Example: Usability |
|---|---|
| Construct | Usability |
| Dimension | Efficiency |
| Indicator | Task completion time |
| Measure | Seconds required to complete a task |
| Data | 45 seconds, 60 seconds, 38 seconds |

## 10. Study Design

### Definition

A study design is the overall plan or blueprint that explains how a research study will answer its research questions.

It specifies:

- What evidence is required.
- From whom or where the evidence will be collected.
- Which methods will be used.
- How variables will be measured.
- How the evidence will be analyzed.
- How validity and ethical considerations will be addressed.

### Importance of Study Design

A suitable study design:

1. Provides a systematic research structure.
2. Aligns methods with research questions.
3. Reduces methodological errors.
4. Improves reliability and validity.
5. Helps manage time and resources.
6. Supports defensible conclusions.

### Main Components of Study Design

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Research Approach | Quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods |
| Research Strategy | Experiment, survey, case study, observation, etc. |
| Population / Sample | Who or what is studied |
| Data Collection | How evidence is obtained |
| Variables / Constructs | What is measured or explored |
| Analysis Plan | How evidence is analyzed |
| Validity / Reliability | How research quality is assessed |
| Ethics | How participants and data are protected |

## 11. Quantitative, Qualitative, and Mixed-Methods Research

### A. Quantitative Research

Quantitative research collects and analyzes numerical data to measure variables, test hypotheses, identify patterns, and examine relationships.

Characteristics:

- Numerical data.
- Structured measurement.
- Statistical analysis.
- Hypothesis testing where appropriate.
- Often uses larger samples depending on the analysis.

Examples:

- Survey ratings.
- Temperature measurements.
- Crop yield values.
- Model accuracy and prediction errors.

### B. Qualitative Research

Qualitative research explores experiences, perceptions, meanings, and behaviors using non-numerical evidence.

Characteristics:

- Textual, audio, or visual data.
- Interviews and observations.
- Open-ended questions.
- Thematic or content analysis.
- Focus on context and depth.

Examples:

- Farmer interviews.
- Focus-group discussions.
- Observation notes.
- User experiences.

### C. Mixed-Methods Research

Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches within one study.

It provides numerical measurement alongside contextual understanding.

### Common Mixed-Methods Designs

| Design | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sequential Explanatory | Quantitative first, qualitative afterward | Survey results followed by interviews |
| Sequential Exploratory | Qualitative first, quantitative afterward | Interviews used to develop a survey |
| Convergent | Both approaches conducted in parallel | Survey and interviews collected together |

### Comparison

| Feature | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Numerical | Textual / descriptive |
| Purpose | Measure and test | Explore and understand |
| Questions | How much? How many? | How? Why? |
| Methods | Surveys, experiments | Interviews, observation |
| Analysis | Statistical | Thematic / content |
| Output | Numerical findings | Themes and interpretations |

## 12. Study Design for Machine Learning and Data Science Research

Machine learning research also requires a clear research design.

A machine learning study should define:

1. Research problem.
2. Prediction or classification task.
3. Dataset and data source.
4. Unit of analysis.
5. Predictor variables.
6. Target variable.
7. Baseline models.
8. Proposed models.
9. Evaluation metrics.
10. Validation strategy.
11. Generalization requirements.
12. Reproducibility and ethical considerations.

### Example

Research problem:

Predicting agricultural crop yield across different districts.

| Component | Example |
|---|---|
| Task | Regression |
| Unit of Analysis | District × crop × season × year |
| Target | Crop yield |
| Predictors | Climate and agroecological features |
| Baseline | Linear Regression |
| ML Models | Random Forest, XGBoost |
| Metrics | RMSE, MAE, R² |
| Validation | Spatial / district-based validation |
| Generalization | Prediction in unseen districts |

### Important ML Research Concepts

**Baseline Model:** A simple reference model used to compare the performance of more advanced methods.

**Train-Test Split:** Dividing data into training and testing subsets.

**Cross-Validation:** Repeatedly dividing data into training and validation subsets to evaluate model performance.

**Data Leakage:** Occurs when information from outside the permitted training process influences model training or evaluation.

**Generalization:** The ability of a model to perform well on new, unseen data.

**Ablation Study:** Evaluates the contribution of individual components or features by removing them from a model or system.

**Important:** Random train-test splitting may produce overly optimistic results when observations from the same geographical area appear in both training and testing sets. Spatial research may require district-based or geographically separated validation.

## 13. Validity and Reliability

Research quality depends on whether a study measures what it intends to measure and produces consistent, defensible findings.

### A. Construct Validity

Construct validity refers to whether a measurement accurately represents the theoretical construct it is intended to measure.

Example:

Using an appropriate validated scale to measure user satisfaction.

### B. Internal Validity

Internal validity refers to whether the observed effect or relationship can reasonably be attributed to the factor being investigated rather than alternative explanations.

Example:

In an experiment, controlling confounding variables improves internal validity.

### C. External Validity

External validity refers to the extent to which research findings can be generalized to other populations, settings, or contexts.

Example:

A model tested only in urban areas may not generalize to rural areas.

### D. Reliability

Reliability refers to the consistency or stability of a measurement or research instrument.

Example:

A reliable questionnaire should produce reasonably consistent results under similar conditions.

### E. Conclusion Validity

Conclusion validity concerns whether the conclusions drawn from the analysis are justified by the available evidence and statistical procedures.

### F. Ethical Quality

Ethical quality concerns whether the research respects participants, protects data, minimizes harm, and follows appropriate ethical procedures.

### Comparison

| Concept | Main Question |
|---|---|
| Construct Validity | Are we measuring the intended concept? |
| Internal Validity | Is the observed effect credible? |
| External Validity | Can findings generalize? |
| Reliability | Are measurements consistent? |
| Conclusion Validity | Are conclusions supported by evidence? |
| Ethical Quality | Was the research conducted responsibly? |

---

## 14. Research Feasibility

### Definition

Research feasibility refers to whether a proposed study can realistically be completed within the available time, resources, skills, and ethical constraints.

### Main Feasibility Factors

1. **Time:** Can the research be completed within the deadline?
2. **Data Availability:** Are the required datasets or participants accessible?
3. **Technical Resources:** Are suitable hardware, software, and tools available?
4. **Research Skills:** Does the researcher have the necessary knowledge?
5. **Financial Resources:** Can the research be conducted within the available budget?
6. **Ethics:** Can the study obtain appropriate permissions and protect participants?
7. **Scope:** Is the project sufficiently focused?

### Example

A deep learning model requiring millions of labeled images may be infeasible for a small course project if the researcher has limited computational resources and insufficient data.

### Feasibility Checklist

- Is the dataset accessible?
- Is the sample size realistic?
- Are the required tools available?
- Is the method technically achievable?
- Is the timeline sufficient?
- Can ethical approval be obtained where necessary?
- Can the expected contribution be demonstrated?

## 15. Alignment Matrix

An alignment matrix checks whether different parts of a research study are logically connected.

### Example

| Research Question | Objective | Evidence | Method | Analysis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RQ1: Does climate information improve yield prediction? | Evaluate climate features | Historical climate and yield data | ML modelling | Compare RMSE and MAE |
| RQ2: Does seasonal modelling improve prediction? | Evaluate seasonal patterns | Crop-season data | Season-specific modelling | Performance comparison |
| RQ3: Can models generalize to unseen districts? | Assess spatial transferability | District-level data | Leave-one-district-out validation | District-wise errors |
| RQ4: Does geographical similarity explain model errors? | Investigate error patterns | Agroclimatic features and prediction errors | Similarity analysis | Statistical and spatial analysis |

### Importance

The alignment matrix helps identify:

- Unanswerable research questions.
- Missing data requirements.
- Inappropriate methods.
- Unclear objectives.
- Unnecessary analyses.

**Important:** Every research question should have an appropriate source of evidence and an analysis method.

## 16. Dataset Selection and Quality Assessment

### Definition

Dataset selection is the process of identifying and evaluating data sources to determine whether they are suitable for answering the research questions.

A dataset should be selected based on research requirements, not popularity alone.

### Dataset Selection Criteria

| Criterion | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Does the dataset match the research problem? |
| Completeness | Are important variables available? |
| Accuracy | Are the observations and labels correct? |
| Consistency | Are formats and measurements consistent? |
| Representativeness | Does the dataset represent the target population or context? |
| Timeliness | Is the data sufficiently recent? |
| Size | Is there enough data for the planned analysis? |
| Licensing | Does the license permit the intended use? |
| Provenance | Is the source and collection process documented? |
| Bias | Does the dataset contain systematic representation problems? |

### Data Quality Dimensions

1. Accuracy.
2. Completeness.
3. Consistency.
4. Validity.
5. Uniqueness.
6. Timeliness.

### Dataset Search Strategy

1. Define research requirements.
2. Search academic databases and repositories.
3. Review dataset papers and documentation.
4. Compare candidate datasets.
5. Check licensing and provenance.
6. Inspect sample records.
7. Select the most appropriate dataset.

### Candidate Dataset Log

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dataset A | Repository | X, Y, Z | Relevant variables | Missing values | Partial fit |
| Dataset B | Research paper | X, Y | Good documentation | Limited coverage | Fit |
| Dataset C | Public platform | X | Large size | Unknown provenance | Reject / investigate |

## 17. Introduction to Human-Centered Research

### Definition

Human-centered research focuses on understanding people's experiences, behaviors, needs, perceptions, and interactions with systems, technologies, or environments.

It involves collecting and analyzing evidence from people or data representing people.

Examples:

- User experience research.
- Human-computer interaction.
- Surveys.
- Interviews.
- Focus-group discussions.
- Usability testing.
- Behavioral observation.

### Main Objective

To understand human experiences and behaviors systematically while respecting participants' rights, privacy, dignity, and safety.

### Continuity from Week 4 to Week 5

Week 4 produced a research design outline. Week 5 converts that design into instruments, an ethics plan, and a responsible data collection process.

The process is:

**Revised RQs + Study Design → Instruments → Ethics Plan → Responsible Data Collection**

### Important Principle

Ethics must shape research instruments and data collection procedures from the beginning. It should not be treated as a final checklist after data collection.

Before an instrument reaches participants or a dataset enters the research pipeline, relevant ethical questions must be addressed in writing.

## 19. Method Selection According to Research Questions

Different research questions require different methods.

A researcher should select methods according to the evidence needed to answer each RQ.

### Method Selection Table

| Research Purpose | Suitable Method |
|---|---|
| Measure opinions or satisfaction | Survey |
| Understand experiences | Interview |
| Explore group attitudes | Focus Group |
| Study actual behavior | Observation |
| Evaluate usability | Usability Testing |
| Analyze existing data | Dataset Analysis |
| Compare model performance | ML Experiments |
| Explore both numerical and contextual evidence | Mixed Methods |

### Method Selection Principle

One research project may use different methods for different research questions.

For example:

- RQ1 may require a survey.
- RQ2 may require interviews.
- RQ3 may require usability testing.

The method should be selected separately for each RQ.

### Fast Method Selection Check

Ask:

1. What evidence is required?
2. Who or what can provide the evidence?
3. Which method can collect it?
4. What are the ethical risks?
5. How will the evidence be analyzed?

## 20. Survey Design

### Definition

A survey is a structured method of collecting information from respondents through a set of standardized questions.

It is commonly used to measure opinions, attitudes, behaviors, experiences, and demographic characteristics.

### Types of Survey Questions

**A. Closed-Ended Questions**

Respondents select from predefined answers.

Example:

"How often do you use the application?"

- Daily.
- Weekly.
- Monthly.
- Never.

**B. Open-Ended Questions**

Respondents answer in their own words.

Example:

"What difficulties do you experience while using the application?"

**C. Likert-Scale Questions**

Respondents indicate their level of agreement or disagreement.

Example:

"I find the system easy to use."

1. Strongly Disagree.
2. Disagree.
3. Neutral.
4. Agree.
5. Strongly Agree.

### Survey Design Process

1. Identify the construct to be measured.
2. Define the target population.
3. Select suitable indicators.
4. Develop questions.
5. Select response scales.
6. Organize the questionnaire.
7. Pilot-test the survey.
8. Revise unclear questions.
9. Finalize the instrument.

### Good Survey Question Characteristics

A good survey question should be:

- Clear.
- Short.
- Specific.
- Neutral.
- Unambiguous.
- Relevant to the research objective.
- Appropriate for the target population.

### Common Survey Question Problems

| Problem | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Leading Question | Influences the response | "Don't you agree this system is excellent?" |
| Double-Barrelled | Asks two things at once | "Is the system fast and easy to use?" |
| Ambiguous | Has unclear meaning | "Do you use the system regularly?" |
| Loaded Question | Contains an assumption | "Why do you dislike the system?" |
| Technical Language | Uses unfamiliar terms | Asking nontechnical users about algorithmic optimization |

### Survey Length and Scale Design

For course-level research:

- Aim for a survey of 10 minutes or less.
- Use validated scales where appropriate.
- Keep response scales consistent.
- Clearly label scale points.
- Include a genuine neutral or "Prefer not to answer" option when appropriate.
- Avoid unnecessary questions.

Examples of established instruments:

- System Usability Scale (SUS).
- NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX).
- Published trust or credibility scales.

**Important:** Additional questions increase respondent effort and may reduce completion rates.

### Pilot Testing

A pilot test is a small preliminary test conducted before the main survey.

A course-level survey may be piloted with approximately 3–5 people from the target population.

The researcher should check:

- Whether questions are understandable.
- Whether response options are appropriate.
- Whether the survey length is reasonable.
- Whether participants interpret questions consistently.
- Whether any question creates discomfort.

## 21. Sampling and Recruitment

### Definition of Sampling

Sampling is the process of selecting a subset of individuals or observations from a larger population for research.

### Population

The entire group that the researcher intends to study.

### Sample

The selected subset of the population from which evidence is collected.

### Common Sampling Techniques

#### A. Convenience Sampling

Participants are selected based on accessibility and availability.

Example:

Recruiting students from the researcher's university.

Advantages:
- Easy.
- Fast.
- Low cost.

Limitations:
- May produce selection bias.
- May not represent the wider population.

#### B. Snowball Sampling

Existing participants help identify or recruit additional participants.

Example:

One farmer introduces the researcher to other farmers.

Advantages:
- Useful for hard-to-reach populations.
- Helps access specialized communities.

Limitations:
- Participants may come from similar social networks.
- Can reduce diversity.

#### C. Purposive Sampling

Participants are selected because they have specific characteristics or relevant experience.

Example:

Selecting experienced agricultural extension officers for interviews.

Advantages:
- Provides information-rich participants.
- Useful for specialized research.

Limitations:
- Requires clear selection criteria.
- Researcher judgment may influence selection.

### Sample Size Considerations

**For Quantitative Research:**

Sample size should depend on:

- Planned statistical analysis.
- Expected effect size.
- Statistical power.
- Significance level.
- Population characteristics.
- Available resources.

**For Qualitative Research:**

Sample size may be guided by:

- Research scope.
- Participant diversity.
- Information richness.
- Thematic saturation.

For a semester project, approximately 8–15 interviews may be a realistic planning range, but this is not a universal requirement.

### Recruitment Ethics

Researchers should avoid coercion during recruitment.

Special care is needed when recruiting:

- Students.
- Juniors.
- Employees.
- Subordinates.
- People dependent on the researcher.

Participants must understand that participation is voluntary and refusal will not result in punishment or disadvantage.

## 22. Semi-Structured Interviews

### Definition

A semi-structured interview is a qualitative data collection method that uses a prepared set of questions while allowing the researcher to ask follow-up questions.

It combines structure with flexibility.

### Main Components of an Interview Guide

1. **Opening:** Introduce the research and explain consent.
2. **Warm-up Questions:** Begin with simple questions.
3. **Core Questions:** Address the main research objectives.
4. **Probes:** Ask follow-up questions for clarification.
5. **Closing:** Invite final comments and thank the participant.

### Example Interview Questions

Research topic: Farmers' experience with weather information systems.

- How do you usually receive weather information?
- Can you describe the last time weather information influenced your farming decision?
- What difficulties did you experience?
- What sources do you trust most?
- Why do you prefer those sources?

### Probing Questions

- Can you explain further?
- Can you give an example?
- What happened next?
- Why did you make that decision?
- How did you feel about the experience?

### Good Interview Question Principles

- Use open-ended questions.
- Avoid leading questions.
- Avoid unnecessary technical language.
- Ask one question at a time.
- Focus on relevant experiences.
- Allow participants to explain their answers.

### Concrete Behaviour vs. Hypothetical Opinion

Researchers should prefer questions about actual recent behavior rather than hypothetical opinions.

Better:

"Walk me through the last time you verified a social media post."

Less informative:

"Would you use a verification tool?"

Concrete experiences generally provide more useful behavioral evidence.

---

## 23. Focus Group Discussions

### Definition

A focus group is a qualitative research method in which a small group of participants discusses a particular topic under the guidance of a moderator.

It is useful for exploring shared opinions, experiences, attitudes, and social interactions.

### Typical Group Size

Approximately 4–8 participants may be suitable for a focus group.

### Roles

**Moderator:**
- Introduces the topic.
- Guides the discussion.
- Encourages participation.
- Prevents domination by one participant.
- Maintains focus.

**Note-Taker:**
- Records important points.
- Notes interactions.
- Observes group dynamics.
- Documents non-verbal communication where relevant.

### Advantages

- Collects multiple perspectives.
- Encourages discussion.
- Reveals agreement and disagreement.
- Helps identify shared social norms.
- Produces rich qualitative data.

### Limitations

- Dominant participants may influence others.
- Some participants may hesitate to speak.
- Group pressure may affect responses.
- Sensitive topics may be difficult to discuss.
- Confidentiality cannot be fully guaranteed.

### When Should a Focus Group Be Used?

Use a focus group when interaction among participants is itself important research evidence.

### Confidentiality Limitation

Researchers can promise to protect information within their control, but they cannot guarantee that other participants will keep the discussion confidential.

This limitation should be clearly explained during informed consent.

---

## 24. Observation

### Definition

Observation is a research method in which researchers systematically watch and record behaviors, activities, or events in their natural or structured settings.

### Types of Observation

#### A. Naturalistic Observation

Researchers observe behavior in its natural environment without manipulating the situation.

Example:

Observing how farmers use mobile applications during normal farming activities.

#### B. Structured Observation

Researchers observe behavior according to a predefined protocol or checklist.

Example:

Recording how many users complete a specific task successfully.

### Advantages

- Captures actual behavior.
- Provides contextual information.
- Helps identify unexpected patterns.
- Reduces reliance on self-reported information.

### Limitations

- Observer bias.
- Observer effect.
- Time-consuming data collection.
- Limited understanding of participants' motivations.

### Observer Effect

The observer effect occurs when people change their behavior because they know they are being observed.

### Observation vs. Interpretation

Researchers should separate what they directly observe from their interpretation.

Example:

Observation: "The participant clicked the wrong button three times."

Interpretation: "The interface may be confusing."

These should be recorded separately.

### Ethical Considerations in Online Observation

Publicly available online information is not automatically ethically unrestricted research data.

Researchers should:

- Consider users' privacy expectations.
- Avoid unnecessary collection of personal information.
- Anonymize identifiable content.
- Avoid searchable verbatim quotations when they could expose individuals.
- Consider whether consent is necessary for the specific context.

### Combining Observation and Interviews

Observation shows what people do, while interviews help explain why they do it.

Combining the two methods can provide complementary evidence.

## 25. Usability Testing

### Definition

Usability testing is a research method used to evaluate how effectively, efficiently, and satisfactorily users can interact with a system or product.

It identifies usability problems through realistic user tasks.

### Main Usability Measures

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Task Success | Whether the participant completes the task |
| Task Completion Time | Time required to complete the task |
| Error Rate | Number or frequency of errors |
| User Satisfaction | Participant's perceived satisfaction |
| SUS Score | Standardized usability measurement |

### Example

A researcher evaluates a mobile agricultural application.

Participants are asked to:

1. Open the application.
2. Find weather information.
3. Locate a crop-related advisory.
4. Complete a specific task.

The researcher records task success, completion time, errors, and user feedback.

### Think-Aloud Method

In the think-aloud method, participants explain their thoughts while performing tasks.

Advantages:
- Reveals user confusion.
- Helps understand decision-making.
- Identifies interface problems.

Limitation:

Thinking aloud may influence normal task performance and affect task completion time.

### Small-Sample Usability Testing

Approximately five users may reveal many usability problems during iterative testing.

However, this does not automatically provide a sufficient sample for hypothesis testing or estimating statistical effects.

**Important Principle:** Test the system, not the participant.

Researchers should avoid making participants feel that they are being evaluated personally.

### Realistic Task Design

Tasks should be goal-based and have clear success criteria.

Weak task:

"Explore the application."

Strong task:

"Find a suspicious post and report it using the application."

## 26. Dataset-Track Research Methods

Researchers working with datasets must also select suitable methods for collecting, preparing, analyzing, and evaluating data.

### Three Common Dataset Types

#### A. Existing Datasets

Datasets already collected and published by another organization or researcher.

Examples:

- Public research datasets.
- Government datasets.
- Benchmark datasets.

#### B. Scraped or Logged Datasets

Data collected from websites, platforms, applications, or system logs.

Examples:

- Public web pages.
- Social media posts.
- Application usage logs.

#### C. Self-Collected and Annotated Datasets

Researchers collect data and assign labels or categories.

Examples:

- Image classification datasets.
- Interview transcripts with coded labels.
- Manually annotated text datasets.

### Dataset-Track Methodological Requirements

Researchers should define:

1. Data source.
2. Unit of analysis.
3. Sampling method.
4. Collection period.
5. Inclusion and exclusion criteria.
6. Annotation procedure.
7. Data cleaning process.
8. Evaluation protocol.
9. Performance metrics.
10. Ethical and licensing constraints.

### Evaluation Protocol

An ML study should specify:

- Train, validation, and test splits.
- Baseline models.
- Proposed models.
- Evaluation metrics.
- Ablation studies where relevant.
- Generalization requirements.

### Common Evaluation Metrics

| Task | Common Metrics |
|---|---|
| Classification | Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score |
| Regression | MAE, RMSE, R² |
| Clustering | Silhouette Score |
| Information Retrieval | Precision@K, Recall@K, MAP |

### Annotation Quality

When collecting labeled data:

- Provide written annotation guidelines.
- Use multiple annotators where feasible.
- Measure inter-annotator agreement.
- Resolve disagreements systematically.
- Document the labeling process.

**Cohen's Kappa:** A statistical measure of agreement between annotators that accounts for agreement expected by chance.

## 27. Dataset Requirement Audit and Selection

Dataset selection should begin with research requirements, not with browsing popular datasets.

### Step 1: Define Dataset Requirements

Researchers should specify:

- Research task.
- Required labels.
- Unit of analysis.
- Target population or domain.
- Data collection conditions.
- Time period.
- Minimum data scale.
- Class balance requirements.
- License and provenance requirements.

### Step 2: Inspect Dataset Documentation

Read:

- Dataset paper.
- Datasheet.
- Collection methodology.
- Licensing information.
- Annotation documentation.
- Known limitations.

### Step 3: Manually Inspect Samples

Inspect approximately 50–100 samples where feasible.

Check:

- Whether the data matches its description.
- Whether labels are correct.
- Whether important classes are missing.
- Whether duplicates exist.
- Whether the data represents the intended domain.

### Step 4: Make a Dataset Decision

| Decision | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fit | Meets the core research requirements |
| Partial Fit | Has limitations that can be honestly mitigated |
| Reject | A core requirement fails and no reasonable mitigation exists |

### Worked Example: Dataset Fit

Research question:

"To what extent can a detection model identify helmet non-use among motorcycle riders in Dhaka daytime traffic images?"

Candidate dataset:

A public helmet dataset collected from Indian urban traffic CCTV.

| Requirement | Dataset Limitation |
|---|---|
| Task and labels | Helmet bounding boxes may not be linked to individual riders |
| Domain | Indian traffic conditions differ from Dhaka |
| Weather | Dataset contains only daytime and dry-weather images |
| Class balance | Approximately 12,000 helmet instances vs. 1,800 no-helmet instances |
| License | CC BY-NC 4.0 |
| Annotation | No clear annotator agreement information |

### Conclusion

The dataset may be classified as a Partial Fit.

Researchers must:

- Document the limitations.
- Address class imbalance.
- Consider domain shift.
- Narrow the generalization claim.
- Respect license restrictions.
- Avoid claiming performance in conditions not represented by the dataset.

**Important:** A popular or large dataset is not necessarily suitable for a specific research question.

## 28. Instrument Quality and Pilot Testing

### Definition

Instrument quality refers to how well a research instrument measures or collects the information it is intended to capture.

Examples of instruments:

- Questionnaires.
- Interview guides.
- Observation checklists.
- Usability task scripts.
- Annotation guidelines.

### Characteristics of a Good Research Instrument

1. Validity.
2. Reliability.
3. Clarity.
4. Consistency.
5. Relevance.
6. Suitability for the target population.
7. Ethical acceptability.

### Pilot Testing

A pilot test is a small preliminary study used to evaluate and improve research instruments before the main data collection.

For a course-level study, approximately 3–5 target-population participants may be used to pilot an instrument.

### What Should Be Checked?

- Do participants understand the questions?
- Do they interpret questions consistently?
- Are any questions confusing?
- Are response scales appropriate?
- Is the instrument too long?
- Does the instrument produce relevant information?
- Are any questions sensitive or uncomfortable?

### Important Principle

If a pilot participant asks what a question means, the researcher should consider that the instrument may have failed to communicate clearly. The participant should not automatically be blamed.

### Language and Translation

For multilingual research:

1. Prepare the original instrument.
2. Translate it into the target language.
3. Consider back-translation.
4. Compare the translated versions.
5. Pilot-test with the target population.
6. Record which language each participant used.

Researchers should ensure that translated questions preserve meaning rather than simply translating words literally.

## 29. Thematic Analysis

### Definition

Thematic analysis is a qualitative data analysis method used to identify, analyze, and interpret patterns or themes within qualitative data.

It is commonly used for interview transcripts, focus-group discussions, and open-ended survey responses.

### Six Phases of Thematic Analysis

#### Phase 1: Familiarization with Data

Researchers read and re-read the data to become familiar with its content.

Activities:
- Read transcripts.
- Listen to recordings.
- Write initial observations.

#### Phase 2: Generating Initial Codes

Researchers identify meaningful segments of data and assign codes.

Example:

Participant statement:

"I stopped using the application because the weather information was often delayed."

Possible code:

"Delayed information."

#### Phase 3: Searching for Themes

Researchers group related codes into broader patterns.

Example:

- Delayed information.
- Incorrect information.
- Missing updates.

Possible theme:

"Trust and reliability concerns."

#### Phase 4: Reviewing Themes

Researchers check whether themes accurately represent the coded data and the complete dataset.

#### Phase 5: Defining and Naming Themes

Researchers refine each theme and describe its central meaning.

#### Phase 6: Producing the Report

Researchers explain the themes using appropriate evidence and quotations.

### Practical Considerations

- One hour of audio may require approximately 4–6 hours of transcription.
- Consent must cover audio or video recording.
- Code data in the original language where feasible.
- Translate themes rather than unnecessarily translating every raw quotation.
- Maintain a record of coding and theme development.

**Important:** Themes are developed through systematic analytical work. They do not simply emerge automatically from data.

## 30. Research Ethics

### Definition

Research ethics refers to the moral principles and professional standards that guide researchers in conducting research responsibly.

It protects participants, data subjects, communities, and the integrity of research.

### Importance of Research Ethics

Research ethics helps to:

1. Protect participants from harm.
2. Respect individual rights and dignity.
3. Ensure voluntary participation.
4. Maintain privacy and confidentiality.
5. Prevent exploitation.
6. Improve research credibility.
7. Promote responsible data use.

---

## 31. Belmont Report: Three Fundamental Ethical Principles

The Belmont Report identifies three major principles for protecting human research participants.

### A. Respect for Persons

This principle recognizes individual autonomy and requires special protection for individuals with limited autonomy.

It includes:

- Voluntary participation.
- Informed consent.
- Respect for participant decisions.
- Protection of vulnerable participants.

Example:

Participants must be free to refuse participation without punishment.

### B. Beneficence

Beneficence means maximizing possible benefits and minimizing potential harm.

Researchers should:

- Identify potential risks.
- Reduce unnecessary risks.
- Protect participants.
- Ensure that expected benefits justify remaining risks.

Example:

A researcher should avoid exposing participants' sensitive personal information.

### C. Justice

Justice concerns fairness in the distribution of research benefits and burdens.

Researchers should avoid unfairly selecting or excluding participants.

Example:

A study should not recruit only disadvantaged people simply because they are easier to access.

### Comparison

| Principle | Main Focus |
|---|---|
| Respect for Persons | Autonomy and informed consent |
| Beneficence | Maximize benefits and minimize harm |
| Justice | Fair participant selection and treatment |

---

## 32. Informed Consent

### Definition

Informed consent is a process through which participants voluntarily agree to take part in research after receiving sufficient information about the study.

Consent should be informed, voluntary, and ongoing where appropriate.

### Elements of Informed Consent

A consent form should explain:

1. Research title and purpose.
2. Researcher's identity and contact information.
3. Why the participant is being invited.
4. What participation involves.
5. Expected duration.
6. Potential risks and benefits.
7. Privacy and confidentiality measures.
8. Data storage and access.
9. Voluntary participation.
10. Right to withdraw.
11. Contact information for questions or concerns.

### Withdrawal Rights

Participants should be informed that they may withdraw from the study without penalty.

The consent document should also explain what happens to data already collected if a participant withdraws.

For example:

- Whether identifiable data can be deleted.
- Whether already-anonymized data can still be removed.
- Whether some data may already have been included in analysis.

### Separate Recording Consent

Permission for audio or video recording should be a separate decision from general participation consent.

A participant may agree to an interview but refuse recording.

### Ongoing Consent

Consent is not necessarily a one-time event. Researchers should clarify important changes in the research process and respect participants' continued willingness to participate.

## 33. Privacy, Confidentiality, Anonymization, and Pseudonymization

### Privacy

Privacy refers to an individual's right to control access to personal information and personal activities.

### Confidentiality

Confidentiality refers to the researcher's responsibility to protect information provided by participants and prevent unauthorized disclosure.

### Anonymization

Anonymization is the process of removing or transforming identifying information so that individuals cannot reasonably be identified.

Example:

Removing names, phone numbers, and other direct identifiers from a dataset.

### Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with codes or artificial identifiers.

Example:

Replacing a participant's name with "P001".

A separate key may connect the code to the participant's real identity.

### Comparison

| Feature | Anonymization | Pseudonymization |
|---|---|---|
| Direct Identifiers | Removed or transformed | Replaced with codes |
| Identity Link | Not reasonably recoverable | Can be recovered using a separate key |
| Re-identification | Intended to be prevented | Possible with the key |
| Example | Fully de-identified records | P001, P002, P003 |

### Data Protection Measures

- Encrypt sensitive files.
- Restrict access.
- Store identity keys separately.
- Use secure storage.
- Define retention periods.
- Delete data when appropriate.
- Avoid sharing identifiable data unnecessarily.

### Indirect Identifiers

Information that does not directly identify a person may identify them when combined.

Example:

- Job role.
- Department.
- Gender.
- Institution.

In a small community, this combination may identify a specific individual.

Researchers should generalize or aggregate such information where necessary.

## 34. Ethics Review and IRB

### Definition

An Institutional Review Board (IRB) is an independent committee that reviews research involving human participants to ensure that ethical requirements are addressed.

### Main Responsibilities of an IRB

1. Review research design.
2. Evaluate potential risks.
3. Assess informed consent.
4. Protect participant rights.
5. Evaluate privacy and confidentiality.
6. Ensure fair participant selection.
7. Review proposed risk mitigation measures.

### Risk-Benefit Assessment

Researchers should identify:

1. The worst realistic harm that could happen.
2. The likelihood of that harm.
3. The potential impact.
4. The measures to prevent or reduce the risk.
5. Whether expected benefits justify the remaining risks.

### Course-Level and Thesis-Level Review

According to the lecture's course-level guidance, the instructor should review the research instruments and ethics considerations before data collection begins.

Formal thesis research may require review through the university's designated ethics process.

**Important:** Researchers should not begin data collection before obtaining the required review or permission.

## 35. Dataset Ethics

### Definition

Dataset ethics concerns the responsible collection, use, management, analysis, and sharing of datasets, especially when they contain information about people or communities.

A dataset may be technically accessible but still raise ethical concerns.

### Major Dataset Ethics Issues

#### A. Data Provenance

Researchers should know:

- Who collected the data.
- Where it came from.
- Why it was collected.
- How it was collected.
- How it was processed.
- Who labeled it.

#### B. Licensing

A dataset license determines the permitted conditions of use.

Researchers should check whether the license allows:

- Research use.
- Commercial use.
- Redistribution.
- Modification.
- Model training.

**Important:** Public availability does not automatically mean unrestricted research or model-training permission.

#### C. Consent and Secondary Use

Data collected for one purpose may not automatically be suitable for another purpose.

Researchers should consider:

- Original collection purpose.
- Participants' expectations.
- Consent conditions.
- Intended secondary use.
- Potential harm.

#### D. Privacy

Researchers should check for:

- Names.
- Phone numbers.
- Addresses.
- Faces.
- Personal identifiers.
- Sensitive attributes.

#### E. Representation and Bias

A dataset may underrepresent certain populations or conditions.

This can produce biased research results or unfair model predictions.

#### F. Downstream Harm

Researchers should consider whether data or models could be used to:

- Discriminate against individuals.
- Expose private information.
- Mislead users.
- Harm vulnerable communities.
- Enable inappropriate surveillance.

### Scraping and API Rules

For scraped or logged data, researchers should:

- Check platform Terms of Service (ToS).
- Check API restrictions.
- Define the sampling period.
- Document search queries.
- Record collection procedures.
- Handle PII at ingestion rather than waiting until after model training.

### Comfort Test

Researchers should consider whether the people represented in a dataset would reasonably be comfortable with the specific intended use.

## 36. Personally Identifiable Information (PII)

### Definition

Personally Identifiable Information (PII) refers to information that can identify an individual directly or indirectly.

### Examples of Direct Identifiers

- Full name.
- Phone number.
- Email address.
- National identification number.
- Passport number.
- Exact home address.

### Examples of Indirect Identifiers

- Age.
- Gender.
- Occupation.
- Department.
- Location.
- Institution.

Several indirect identifiers may combine to identify an individual.

### Risks of PII Exposure

- Identity theft.
- Privacy violations.
- Harassment.
- Discrimination.
- Financial harm.
- Reputational damage.

### PII Protection

- Collect only necessary information.
- Remove unnecessary identifiers.
- Use pseudonymization where appropriate.
- Restrict access.
- Encrypt sensitive information.
- Establish retention and deletion rules.
- Avoid uploading identifiable information to unauthorized third-party services.

## 37. Data Provenance and Documentation

### Definition

Data provenance refers to the documented history of a dataset, including its origin, collection, processing, transformation, and use.

It helps establish the credibility, traceability, and reproducibility of research data.

### Main Components of Data Provenance

1. Data origin.
2. Collection purpose.
3. Collection method.
4. Sampling strategy.
5. Data collection period.
6. License.
7. Consent basis.
8. Annotation procedure.
9. Data cleaning and preprocessing.
10. Version history.
11. Known limitations.
12. Access and storage conditions.

### Annotation Provenance

For annotated datasets, researchers should document:

- Annotation guidelines.
- Annotator instructions.
- Number of annotators.
- Agreement measures.
- Disagreement resolution.
- Annotation labor and payment conditions where relevant.

### Transformation Log

Every major data transformation should be recorded.

Examples:

- Missing-value handling.
- Duplicate removal.
- Filtering.
- Data normalization.
- Label correction.
- Class rebalancing.
- Feature engineering.

### Why Is Provenance Important?

It helps researchers:

- Verify data reliability.
- Understand dataset limitations.
- Reproduce experiments.
- Identify possible bias.
- Support responsible reuse.

**Important:** Unknown provenance is a research liability. Researchers should explicitly document what is unknown instead of making assumptions.

## 38. Responsible AI Use in Research

### Definition

Responsible AI use means applying artificial intelligence tools in ways that are transparent, ethical, secure, and consistent with research integrity.

### Acceptable Uses of AI Assistance

Depending on institutional and publication policies, AI tools may support:

- Grammar improvement.
- Language editing.
- Brainstorming.
- Code assistance.
- Data analysis support.
- Summarizing material for personal study.
- Improving document organization.

### Uses Requiring Special Care

Researchers should not:

- Upload identifiable participant data to third-party AI tools without authorization and appropriate safeguards.
- Upload raw interview transcripts containing sensitive information without permission.
- Treat synthetic participants as real research evidence.
- Present AI-generated claims as verified facts.
- Use AI-generated references without checking the original sources.
- Hide substantive AI assistance when disclosure is required.

### Human Responsibility

The researcher remains responsible for:

- Accuracy.
- Research decisions.
- Interpretation.
- Ethical compliance.
- Citations.
- Final conclusions.

### AI Disclosure

AI assistance should be disclosed in the methods, acknowledgments, or another required section according to the relevant institutional and publication policies.

**Important:** AI can assist research, but it cannot replace the researcher's responsibility.

# PART C: IMPORTANT COMPARISONS FOR EXAM

## 43. Quantitative vs. Qualitative Research

| Feature | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| Data Type | Numerical | Textual / descriptive |
| Purpose | Measure and test | Explore and understand |
| Research Questions | How much? How many? | How? Why? |
| Methods | Surveys, experiments | Interviews, observation |
| Analysis | Statistical | Thematic / content |
| Sample | Depends on statistical design | Depends on information richness and saturation |
| Output | Numerical findings | Themes and interpretations |

## 44. Interview vs. Focus Group

| Feature | Interview | Focus Group |
|---|---|---|
| Participants | Usually one at a time | Small group |
| Interaction | Researcher and participant | Researcher and group |
| Purpose | Individual experiences | Shared views and group dynamics |
| Depth | Individual depth | Multiple perspectives |
| Risk | Interviewer influence | Group pressure and dominant voices |
| Confidentiality | Easier to manage | Cannot be guaranteed among participants |

## 45. Survey vs. Interview

| Feature | Survey | Interview |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Usually standardized | Flexible |
| Data | Mainly structured responses | Detailed verbal responses |
| Sample | Often larger | Often smaller |
| Analysis | Statistical / descriptive | Thematic |
| Main Advantage | Efficient collection | Rich contextual understanding |
| Main Limitation | Limited depth | Time-consuming |

## 46. Anonymization vs. Pseudonymization

| Feature | Anonymization | Pseudonymization |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Intended to be unrecoverable | Can be recovered with a separate key |
| Identifiers | Removed or transformed | Replaced with codes |
| Example | De-identified records | P001, P002 |
| Re-identification | Not reasonably possible | Possible with additional information |

## 47. H0 vs. H1

| Feature | Null Hypothesis (H0) | Alternative Hypothesis (H1) |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | No relevant effect or difference | Relevant effect or difference exists |
| Role | Default hypothesis for testing | Competing hypothesis |
| Example | No difference between two models | A difference exists between two models |

## 48. Problem Statement vs. Research Question vs. Hypothesis

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Statement | Explains the research problem and gap | Models may not generalize across districts |
| Research Question | Asks what the study will investigate | How well do models generalize to unseen districts? |
| Hypothesis | Predicts a testable outcome | Climate-similar training districts will produce lower prediction errors |

## 49. Validity vs. Reliability

| Feature | Validity | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Measures what it intends to measure | Produces consistent results |
| Main Question | Is the measurement correct? | Is the measurement consistent? |
| Example | A usability scale measures usability | The scale produces consistent results |

## 50. Privacy vs. Confidentiality

| Feature | Privacy | Confidentiality |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Control over personal information | Responsibility to protect collected information |
| Focus | Individual rights | Researcher's obligations |
| Example | Participant chooses not to disclose income | Researcher does not share participant's income |

## 51. Observation vs. Interview

| Feature | Observation | Interview |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Actual behavior | Experiences and explanations |
| Evidence | Actions and events | Verbal responses |
| Strength | Captures behavior in context | Explains motivations |
| Limitation | May not explain why | May contain recall or social desirability bias |

## 52. Dataset Ethics vs. Human-Participant Ethics

| Feature | Human-Participant Ethics | Dataset Ethics |
|---|---|---|

# PART D: EXAM-READY SHORT QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS

## 53. Short Questions — Week 4

### Q1. What is a problem statement?

A problem statement is a clear description of a research problem that explains its context, specific issue, existing evidence, research gap, and research direction.

### Q2. What is a research gap?

A research gap is an unanswered question, limitation, or insufficiently explored area in existing research that justifies conducting a new study.

### Q3. What is a research question?

A research question is a focused and researchable question that defines what a study intends to investigate.

### Q4. What are the characteristics of a good research question?

A good research question should be clear, focused, researchable, feasible, relevant, ethical, and aligned with the research problem.

### Q5. What is a hypothesis?

A hypothesis is a tentative and testable statement predicting a relationship, difference, or effect between variables.

### Q6. What is a null hypothesis?

A null hypothesis (H0) states that there is no relevant relationship, difference, or effect in the population being studied.

### Q7. What is an alternative hypothesis?

An alternative hypothesis (H1) states that a relevant relationship, difference, or effect exists.

### Q8. What is an independent variable?

An independent variable is a predictor or explanatory factor that is examined in relation to an outcome.

### Q9. What is a dependent variable?

A dependent variable is the outcome or response variable that the researcher intends to explain or predict.

### Q10. What is a construct?

A construct is an abstract theoretical concept that cannot always be directly observed, such as trust, satisfaction, or motivation.

### Q11. What is an operational definition?

An operational definition specifies exactly how a construct or variable will be measured in a research study.

### Q12. What is study design?

Study design is the overall blueprint of a research study that specifies the methods, data sources, measurements, and analysis procedures.

### Q13. What is quantitative research?

Quantitative research collects and analyzes numerical data to measure variables, test hypotheses, and identify patterns or relationships.

### Q14. What is qualitative research?

Qualitative research explores experiences, perceptions, meanings, and behaviors using non-numerical evidence.

### Q15. What is mixed-methods research?

Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches to obtain both numerical and contextual evidence.

### Q16. What is construct validity?

Construct validity refers to whether a measurement accurately represents the theoretical construct it is intended to measure.

### Q17. What is internal validity?

Internal validity refers to whether an observed effect or relationship can reasonably be attributed to the factor being investigated.

### Q18. What is external validity?

External validity refers to the extent to which research findings can be generalized to other populations, settings, or contexts.

### Q19. What is reliability?

Reliability refers to the consistency or stability of a measurement or research instrument.

### Q20. What is an alignment matrix?

An alignment matrix connects research questions with objectives, evidence sources, methods, and analysis procedures to ensure methodological consistency.

### Q21. What is data leakage?

Data leakage occurs when information that should not be available during model training influences the model or its evaluation, producing misleading performance results.

### Q22. What is a baseline model?

A baseline model is a simple reference model used to compare the performance of more advanced models.

## 54. Short Questions — Week 5

### Q1. What is human-centered research?

Human-centered research investigates people's experiences, behaviors, needs, and interactions with systems or environments while respecting their rights and safety.

### Q2. What are the two evidence tracks in Week 5?

The two evidence tracks are the human-participant track and the dataset/ML track.

### Q3. What is a survey?

A survey is a structured method of collecting information from respondents using standardized questions.

### Q4. What is a Likert scale?

A Likert scale is a rating scale used to measure attitudes or opinions through ordered response options, such as strongly disagree to strongly agree.

### Q5. What is a pilot test?

A pilot test is a small preliminary test used to identify problems and improve research instruments before the main data collection.

### Q6. What is convenience sampling?

Convenience sampling selects participants based on their accessibility and availability.

### Q7. What is snowball sampling?

Snowball sampling is a technique in which existing participants help recruit additional participants.

### Q8. What is purposive sampling?

Purposive sampling selects participants based on specific characteristics or experiences relevant to the research objectives.

### Q9. What is a semi-structured interview?

A semi-structured interview uses prepared questions while allowing follow-up questions to explore participants' experiences in greater depth.

### Q10. What is a focus group?

A focus group is a qualitative method in which a small group discusses a topic under the guidance of a moderator.

### Q11. What is naturalistic observation?

Naturalistic observation involves studying behavior in its normal environment without manipulating the situation.

### Q12. What is structured observation?

Structured observation uses predefined categories, criteria, or checklists to systematically record behavior.

### Q13. What is the observer effect?

The observer effect occurs when people change their behavior because they know they are being observed.

### Q14. What is usability testing?

Usability testing evaluates how effectively, efficiently, and satisfactorily users interact with a system through realistic tasks.

### Q15. What is the think-aloud method?

The think-aloud method asks participants to verbalize their thoughts while performing tasks to reveal their reasoning and difficulties.

### Q16. What is thematic analysis?

Thematic analysis is a qualitative method used to identify, analyze, and interpret patterns or themes in qualitative data.

### Q17. What is informed consent?

Informed consent is the voluntary agreement of a participant to join a research study after receiving sufficient information about its purpose, procedures, risks, and rights.

### Q18. What is the Belmont Report?

The Belmont Report is a foundational research ethics document that identifies three principles: respect for persons, beneficence, and justice.

### Q19. What is anonymization?

Anonymization is the process of removing or transforming identifying information so that individuals cannot reasonably be identified.

### Q20. What is pseudonymization?

Pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with artificial identifiers or codes while keeping the possibility of reconnecting them to identities through separate information.

### Q21. What is an IRB?

An Institutional Review Board is an independent committee that reviews research involving human participants to protect their rights, safety, and welfare.

### Q22. What is PII?

Personally Identifiable Information refers to information that can identify an individual directly or indirectly.

### Q23. What is data provenance?

Data provenance is the documented history of a dataset, including its origin, collection, processing, transformation, and use.

### Q24. What is dataset ethics?

Dataset ethics concerns the responsible collection, management, analysis, and use of data while addressing privacy, licensing, consent, bias, and potential harm.

### Q25. What is responsible AI use in research?

Responsible AI use means applying AI tools transparently and ethically while protecting data, verifying outputs, following applicable policies, and maintaining researcher accountability.

### Q26. Why is dataset popularity not enough to establish suitability?

A popular dataset may not match the research domain, population, labels, conditions, or licensing requirements. Suitability must be evaluated against explicit research requirements.

### Q27. Why should researchers avoid uploading identifiable participant data to third-party AI tools?

Because doing so may expose private information, violate consent conditions, and create unauthorized data-sharing risks.

### Q28. Why is separate recording consent important?

Because participants may agree to participate in a study but may not wish to have their audio or video recorded. Recording permission should therefore be a separate decision.

# PART F: IMPORTANT EXAM WRITING TIPS

## 57. How to Write a Good Exam Answer

For a definition-based question:

1. Start with a clear definition.
2. Explain the main concept.
3. Mention important characteristics.
4. Give one relevant example.

For a comparison question:

1. Define both concepts briefly.
2. Use a comparison table.
3. Mention the key difference.
4. Add an example if necessary.

For a methodology question:

1. Identify the research purpose.
2. Select an appropriate method.
3. Explain why the method is suitable.
4. Describe how data will be collected.
5. Explain how the data will be analyzed.
6. Mention ethical considerations.

For an application-based question:

1. Understand the research scenario.
2. Identify the research question.
3. Identify the relevant variables or constructs.
4. Select the appropriate method.
5. Explain the expected evidence.
6. Discuss limitations and ethical risks.

Week 5 focuses on implementing that plan responsibly: selecting research instruments, recruiting participants or auditing datasets, obtaining consent where required, protecting privacy, documenting data provenance, conducting ethical reviews, and using AI responsibly.
