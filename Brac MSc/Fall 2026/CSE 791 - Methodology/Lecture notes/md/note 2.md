# 1. Introduction: Research Questions, Hypotheses, and Study Design

### Lecture Overview

Week 4 focuses on revising an initial research idea and transforming it into a literature-aligned, feasible, and defendable study plan.

A researcher should not finalize the research questions immediately after selecting a topic. The initial problem statement and research questions must be reviewed using the findings of the literature review.

### Main Concept

**Initial Problem Statement + Initial Research Questions + Week 3 Literature Review = Revised, Defendable Study Plan**

বাংলায়: প্রথমে একটি research idea তৈরি করা হয়। এরপর literature review থেকে বোঝা যায় কোন বিষয়গুলো আগে গবেষণা হয়েছে এবং কোথায় knowledge gap রয়েছে। সেই gap অনুযায়ী problem statement ও research questions সংশোধন করতে হয়।

### Learning Outcomes

By the end of this session, students should be able to:

1. Revise the problem statement using evidence from previous research.
2. Improve research questions by removing broad or unanswerable questions.
3. Identify hypotheses when applicable.
4. Map research constructs and variables.
5. Select an appropriate research design and methodology.
6. Identify suitable datasets or evidence sources.
7. Evaluate feasibility and validity threats.

### Expected Outputs

- Revised problem statement
- Revised research questions
- Hypotheses, if applicable
- Variable and construct map
- Study design outline
- Evidence or dataset source note

# 2. From Initial Idea to Revised Study Design

Week 4 connects the initial research idea with the literature review and final study design.

### Four Stages

1. **Initial Draft:** Prepare the initial problem statement and research questions.
2. **Literature Evidence:** Conduct literature searches, prepare a literature matrix, and identify research gaps.
3. **Week 4 Revision:** Rewrite the problem statement and research questions using the literature evidence.
4. **Study Design:** Select the evidence source, dataset or participants, research method, analysis technique, and validity considerations.

### Most Important Rule

Every revised Research Question (RQ) must:

- Point backward to a specific literature gap.
- Point forward to a specific evidence source.

**Gap → Research Question → Evidence**

বাংলায়:
- Gap বলে দেয় কী জানা বাকি আছে।
- Research Question বলে দেয় কী জানতে চাই।
- Evidence বলে দেয় কোন data দিয়ে উত্তর বের করব।

### Detective Analogy

| Research Concept | Detective Analogy |
|---|---|
| Literature gap | The mystery |
| Research question | The investigation |
| Evidence source | The clues |

Without a research gap, there is no reason to investigate. Without evidence, the research question cannot be answered.

# 3. Problem Statement Revision

## 3.1 What Is a Problem Statement?

A problem statement is a concise description of a specific research problem, its context, existing knowledge, research gap, and intended investigation.

বাংলায়: Problem statement হলো গবেষণার মূল সমস্যাটি কী, আগে কী জানা গেছে, কী জানা বাকি এবং গবেষণায় কী পরীক্ষা করা হবে—তার সংক্ষিপ্ত ব্যাখ্যা।

## 3.2 Revision Logic

A researcher should not automatically discard the initial research topic. Instead, the initial idea should be improved using literature evidence.

### What to Keep

- Core research domain
- Research motivation
- Real-world problem
- Promising research direction

### What to Remove

- Fake novelty
- Emotional or unsupported claims
- Broad slogans
- Unanswerable research questions
- Claims that cannot be completed within the available timeline

### What to Change

- Research scope
- Target population
- Research method
- Variables and constructs
- Evidence or dataset source

### What to Add

- Evidence supporting the research gap
- Operational definitions
- Feasibility limitations
- Dataset or participant source plan

**Important:** A research question is not useful if previous literature has already solved the problem or the required evidence cannot be collected.

## 3.3 Weak vs. Strong Problem Statement

**Weak Problem Statement:**

"Students use AI tools, so we want to study how AI affects learning."

Problems:
- The population is too broad.
- The outcome is vague.
- No research gap is identified.
- No evidence collection plan is mentioned.

**Revised Problem Statement:**

"Prior work studies AI adoption, but less is known about how scaffold type influences independent problem solving among undergraduate programmers."

Why is it better?

- Identifies existing research.
- Indicates a research gap.
- Specifies the population.
- Identifies the research construct.
- Suggests a measurable research direction.

## 3.4 Problem Statement Formula

**Formula:**

[Context] faces [specific problem]. Prior work shows [known evidence], but [research gap]. Therefore, this study examines [focused phenomenon] using [evidence/method].

**Example:**

"Undergraduate programming students increasingly use AI-based learning tools. Previous studies have examined AI adoption, but limited evidence exists regarding how different AI support types influence independent problem-solving ability. Therefore, this study investigates the relationship between AI scaffolding and independent problem-solving among undergraduate programming students using surveys and performance-based assessments."

# 4. Research Questions (RQs)

## 4.1 What Is a Research Question?

A Research Question (RQ) is a clear, focused, and answerable question that defines what a research study intends to investigate.

**Exam-ready definition:**

A research question is a focused and researchable question that identifies the specific issue a study aims to investigate and guides the selection of evidence, methodology, and analysis.

বাংলায়: Research Question হলো গবেষণায় ঠিক কী বিষয় অনুসন্ধান করা হবে তার নির্দিষ্ট প্রশ্ন। এই প্রশ্নের উত্তর অবশ্যই data বা evidence দিয়ে বের করা সম্ভব হতে হবে।

## 4.2 Characteristics of Weak Research Questions

A weak research question may be:

1. **Too Broad:** Covers an excessively large topic.
2. **Opinion-Based:** Depends mainly on personal opinions.
3. **Solution-Oriented:** Assumes a solution before investigating the problem.
4. **Unanswerable:** Has no clear evidence collection path.
5. **Unclear:** Does not specify population, context, or outcome.

Examples:

- How can AI improve education?
- Is online learning better?
- Why should we build an app?
- How can we solve misinformation?

## 4.3 Characteristics of Strong Research Questions

A strong RQ should have:

- A clearly defined population or context.
- A specific phenomenon or problem.
- Clear constructs or variables.
- An identifiable evidence source.
- A suitable research method.
- A realistic scope and meaningful contribution.

**Exam-ready answer:**

A strong research question should be clear, focused, researchable, and aligned with the literature gap. It must identify the study context, relevant constructs or variables, and the evidence required to answer it.

## 4.4 Anatomy of a Strong Research Question

A strong RQ consists of five important elements:

| Element | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Population/Context | Who or what is studied? | Undergraduate students |
| Phenomenon/Problem | What issue is examined? | AI-assisted learning |
| Constructs/Variables | Which concepts matter? | AI support and learning independence |
| Evidence Signal | What data can answer it? | Survey and test scores |
| Contribution Direction | What will the study explain or evaluate? | Effect of AI support type |

### Research Question Template

"How / To what extent / Why does [X] affect, relate to, or shape [Y] among [population/context] using [evidence or method]?"

## 4.5 Six-Point Research Question Revision Checklist

Every revised RQ should pass six checks.

| Check | Key Question |
|---|---|
| Gap Link | Which literature gap supports this RQ? |
| Evidence | What exact data will answer it? |
| Method Fit | Does the research method match the question? |
| Scope | Is the population, context, dataset, or artifact clear? |
| Construct Clarity | Are the key concepts measurable or interpretable? |
| Feasibility | Can the study be completed within the available time? |

**Important:** It is better to have fewer strong research questions than many weak research questions.

# 5. Types of Research Questions

Different research questions require different evidence and research methods.

| RQ Type | Main Question | Likely Evidence/Method |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What is happening? | Survey, logs, content analysis |
| Comparative | How do groups differ? | Experiment, survey, statistical test |
| Relational | How are X and Y associated? | Correlation, regression, SEM |
| Causal | Does X cause a change in Y? | Experiment, quasi-experiment |
| Interpretive | How do users experience X? | Interview, observation, thematic analysis |
| Design/Evaluation | Does artifact X improve Y? | Prototype, usability test, field study |

### Explanation of Each Type

**1. Descriptive Research Question**

Describes the characteristics, frequency, or current condition of a phenomenon.

Example: What types of misinformation exposure do university students report?

Methods: Survey, logs, and content analysis.

**2. Comparative Research Question**

Compares two or more groups or conditions.

Example: How does learning performance differ between students using AI-assisted learning and traditional learning?

Methods: Experiment, survey, and statistical tests.

**3. Relational Research Question**

Investigates the association between two or more variables.

Example: What is the relationship between AI usage frequency and academic performance?

Methods: Correlation, regression, and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM).

**4. Causal Research Question**

Investigates whether one variable produces a change in another variable.

Example: Does step-by-step AI support improve independent problem-solving performance?

Methods: Controlled experiment or quasi-experiment.

**5. Interpretive Research Question**

Explores people's experiences, perceptions, or interpretations of a phenomenon.

Example: How do students experience AI-assisted programming education?

Methods: Interviews, observations, and thematic analysis.

**6. Design/Evaluation Research Question**

Evaluates whether a developed system, prototype, or intervention achieves its intended purpose.

Example: Does a proposed learning application improve programming task completion?

Methods: Prototype development, usability testing, and field study.

# 6. Worked Example: Revising Research Questions

### Initial Research Question

"How does misinformation affect people?"

Problems:
- No specific population.
- No clear context.
- No measurable constructs.
- No methodology.
- No evidence source.

### Revised Research Questions

**RQ1:** What types of misinformation exposure do students report during the event?

**RQ2:** How are misinformation exposure, perceived credibility, and verification behavior related?

**RQ3:** What contextual constraints shape whether students verify or share information?

### Design Implication

| Research Question | Suitable Method |
|---|---|
| RQ1 | Survey |
| RQ2 | Survey and statistical analysis |
| RQ3 | Interviews or open-ended responses |

**Key Lesson:** Mixed methods should be selected because the research questions require different types of evidence, not simply to make a report longer.

# 7. Hypotheses

## 7.1 What Is a Hypothesis?

A hypothesis is a testable prediction or expectation about a relationship or difference between variables.

**Exam-ready definition:**

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement that predicts an expected relationship or difference between variables based on existing theory or previous research.

বাংলায়: Hypothesis হলো গবেষণায় আগে থেকে তৈরি করা একটি পরীক্ষাযোগ্য ধারণা, যেখানে বলা হয় একটি variable পরিবর্তন হলে অন্য variable কীভাবে পরিবর্তিত হতে পারে।

## 7.2 When Should We Use a Hypothesis?

Use hypotheses when:

- Previous literature supports an expected relationship.
- Variables can be measured.
- Statistical testing is planned.
- The expected direction or difference is reasonably clear.

Example of a weak hypothesis:

"AI will improve learning."

It is vague and does not identify measurable variables or comparison conditions.

Better hypothesis:

"Students using step-by-step AI scaffolds will complete more problems independently than students using answer-only AI support."

## 7.3 When Should We Avoid a Hypothesis?

Avoid forcing hypotheses when:

- The study is exploratory.
- The study is interpretive or qualitative.
- The research is design-first.
- The direction of the relationship is unknown.
- There is insufficient theoretical or empirical support.

**Important:** Not every research study requires a hypothesis.

## 7.4 Types of Hypotheses

### Alternative Hypothesis (H1)

H1 states that a significant relationship or difference is expected between variables.

### Null Hypothesis (H0)

H0 states that there is no significant relationship or difference between the variables.

### Example

Research Question:

"To what extent do provenance cues affect users' willingness to share crisis-related social media posts?"

**H1:** Posts with visible provenance cues will produce lower sharing intention than posts without provenance cues.

**H0:** There is no difference in sharing intention between posts with and without provenance cues.

## 7.5 Structure of a Good Hypothesis

A good hypothesis should identify four elements:

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Independent Variable (IV) | What changes or differs |
| Dependent Variable (DV) | What outcome is measured |
| Direction | Expected increase, decrease, or relationship |
| Comparison | Compared with what condition or group |

### Example

"Students using step-by-step AI scaffolds will achieve higher independent problem-solving scores than students using answer-only AI support."

- IV: AI support type
- DV: Independent problem-solving score
- Direction: Higher score
- Comparison: Step-by-step support vs. answer-only support

# 8. Variables and Constructs

## 8.1 What Is a Construct?

A construct is an abstract concept that researchers want to study but cannot always observe or measure directly.

Examples:
- Trust
- Motivation
- Cognitive load
- Learning independence
- Usability
- Perceived credibility

## 8.2 What Is a Variable?

A variable is a measurable characteristic or factor that can take different values across individuals, groups, or observations.

Examples:
- Age
- Test score
- Time spent on a task
- Number of hints used
- AI usage frequency

## 8.3 Construct vs. Variable

| Construct | Variable/Measure |
|---|---|
| Abstract concept | Observable or measurable value |
| Trust | Trust scale score |
| Motivation | Motivation questionnaire score |
| Cognitive load | NASA-TLX score |
| Learning independence | Independent task completion score |

## 8.4 From Construct to Measurement

Research constructs must be converted into observable indicators and measurable variables.

Example:

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Construct | Trust |
| Dimension | Credibility, reliability, confidence |
| Indicator | Agreement with scale items |
| Measure | 1–5 Likert score |
| Analysis | Mean, regression, group comparison |

## 8.5 What Is Operationalization?

Operationalization is the process of defining an abstract construct in terms of specific observable indicators or measurable variables.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Operationalization is the process of translating abstract research concepts into measurable or observable variables so that they can be systematically investigated.

বাংলায়: Trust বা motivation সরাসরি দেখা যায় না। তাই questionnaire score, task behavior বা অন্য observable evidence দিয়ে এগুলো মাপার নিয়ম নির্ধারণ করাই operationalization।

## 8.6 Examples of Operationalization

| Construct | Operational Definition | Possible Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive Load | Mental effort during a task | NASA-TLX, time on task, errors |
| Trust | Confidence in content or system | Trust scale, sharing choice, verification action |
| Usability | Ease and effectiveness of use | SUS, task success, think-aloud notes |
| Learning Independence | Ability to solve without direct answer dependency | Hint count, retry count, post-test score |

### Possible Measurement Risks

- Self-report bias
- Stated behavior may differ from actual behavior.
- Small sample size
- Prior skill may influence the result.

**Important Question:** What exactly will count as evidence for this construct in my study?

# 9. Evidence Sources

Before selecting a research method, researchers must identify what evidence is available.

There are three major evidence categories.

## 9.1 Human Evidence

Evidence collected directly from human participants.

Examples:
- Survey responses
- Interviews
- Focus groups
- Observations
- Usability testing sessions
- Think-aloud notes

## 9.2 Digital Trace Evidence

Evidence generated through digital activities or systems.

Examples:
- Application logs
- Social media posts
- App reviews
- Screenshots
- Issue reports
- Interaction records
- API metadata

## 9.3 Existing Dataset Evidence

Previously collected and available datasets.

Examples:
- Public datasets
- Institutional records
- Benchmark datasets
- Open research data
- Curated research corpora

### Important Rule

A research method should not be selected before checking whether the required evidence is:

- Accessible
- Legally usable
- Ethically acceptable
- Relevant to the research questions
- Sufficient in quality

For data-dependent projects, students should identify 2–3 possible datasets or evidence sources before finalizing the selection.

# 10. Study Design Selection

## 10.1 What Is Research Design?

Research design is the overall plan or framework that specifies how a research study will collect, measure, and analyze evidence to answer its research questions.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Research design is a structured plan that connects research questions with data sources, methods of data collection, analysis techniques, and validity considerations.

বাংলায়: Research design হলো research question-এর উত্তর বের করার সম্পূর্ণ পরিকল্পনা—কোন data লাগবে, কোথা থেকে আসবে, কীভাবে collect হবে এবং কীভাবে analyze হবে।

## 10.2 Design Follows the Research Question

A method is appropriate only when it can produce evidence that answers the research question.

| Research Purpose | Main Goal |
|---|---|
| Explore | Understand an unknown phenomenon |
| Test | Estimate a causal effect |
| Measure | Estimate prevalence or relationships |
| Explain | Understand associations or mechanisms |
| Build | Create and evaluate an artifact |

### Study Design Selection Checklist

1. Does the design answer every RQ?
2. Are the constructs observable?
3. Is the sample or dataset accessible?
4. Can the results survive basic validity criticism?
5. Can the study be completed within the available timeline?

# 11. Quantitative Research Designs

## 11.1 What Is Quantitative Research?

Quantitative research uses numerical data and statistical techniques to measure variables, identify patterns, compare groups, and test hypotheses.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Quantitative research is a systematic research approach that collects and analyzes numerical data to measure variables, examine relationships, compare groups, and test hypotheses.

### Major Quantitative Designs

**1. Survey**

Used to measure attitudes, behaviors, opinions, and prevalence.

Requires:
- Valid questionnaire items
- Appropriate sampling strategy
- Reliable measurement scales

**2. Quasi-Experiment**

Compares naturally occurring groups or before-and-after conditions without full random assignment.

Requires:
- Comparison groups or time periods
- Control of potential confounding variables

**3. Experiment**

Investigates causal relationships by manipulating an independent variable and measuring its effect on a dependent variable.

Requires:
- Experimental conditions
- A comparison or control condition
- Randomization where possible

**4. Dataset/ML Study**

Uses existing or collected datasets to predict, classify, detect, or model patterns.

Requires:
- Appropriate dataset
- Data quality assessment
- Baseline models
- Evaluation metrics
- Proper train/test strategy

# 12. Qualitative Research Designs

## 12.1 What Is Qualitative Research?

Qualitative research investigates people's experiences, perceptions, meanings, and behaviors using non-numerical evidence such as interviews, observations, and textual data.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Qualitative research is an approach that explores human experiences, meanings, perceptions, and social processes through non-numerical data and interpretive analysis.

### Major Qualitative Methods

**1. Interviews**

Used to understand:
- Personal experiences
- Reasoning
- Perceptions
- Decision-making processes

**2. Observation**

Used to examine actual behavior in natural or structured settings.

**3. Focus Groups**

Used to understand:
- Shared opinions
- Group norms
- Disagreements
- Collective meaning

**4. Thematic Analysis**

Identifies and interprets recurring patterns or themes in qualitative data such as interview transcripts and open-ended responses.

# 13. Mixed-Methods Research

## 13.1 What Is Mixed-Methods Research?

Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches within one study to develop a more comprehensive understanding of a research problem.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Mixed-methods research is a research approach that integrates quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis to answer research questions that cannot be fully addressed by a single method.

## 13.2 Types of Mixed-Methods Design

### 1. Sequential Explanatory Design

**QUAN → qual**

- First, quantitative data are collected and analyzed.
- Then, qualitative data are collected to explain the quantitative findings.

Example: A survey identifies unexpected student behavior, and interviews explain why it occurs.

### 2. Sequential Exploratory Design

**qual → QUAN**

- First, qualitative research explores a phenomenon.
- Then, quantitative research measures the identified patterns on a larger scale.

Example: Interviews identify barriers to online learning, and a survey measures how common those barriers are.

### 3. Convergent Design

**QUAN + qual**

- Quantitative and qualitative data are collected in parallel.
- Both findings are analyzed and compared.

Example: A survey and interviews are conducted during the same research period to study user satisfaction.

### Comparison

| Design | Sequence | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Sequential Explanatory | QUAN → qual | Explain quantitative results |
| Sequential Exploratory | qual → QUAN | Explore first, measure later |
| Convergent | QUAN + qual | Compare and integrate both findings |

**Important:** Mixed methods should be used when different types of evidence answer different parts of the research questions. It should not be added merely to make a report longer.

# 14. ML, Computer Vision, and Data Science Research Design

ML, CV, and data science studies have a different design logic from traditional participant-based research.

The central question is:

"What can the model learn, and how do we know it works?"

There are four major components.

## 14.1 Task Framing

Define the research task precisely.

Examples:
- Prediction
- Classification
- Object detection
- Generation

The task definition operationalizes the research question.

## 14.2 Dataset as Study Design

Dataset selection is a research design decision, not merely a preprocessing activity.

It determines:
- What the model can learn.
- Which population or domain is represented.
- Which conditions are excluded.
- Whether the research findings are generalizable.

## 14.3 Evaluation Protocol

Define how model performance will be evaluated.

Examples of metrics:

| Task | Possible Metrics |
|---|---|
| Classification | Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score |
| Object Detection | mAP |
| Text Generation | BLEU |
| Regression | MAE, RMSE, R² |

Baselines and ablation studies are important for evaluating model performance and understanding the contribution of different components.

## 14.4 Generalization Claim

A researcher must clearly state the population, domain, or conditions to which the model is expected to generalize.

Example:

A model trained on urban daytime traffic images should not automatically claim reliable performance in rural nighttime conditions.

### ML Research Design Alignment

| Traditional Research | ML/Data Science Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Research Question | Task framing |
| Sample/Participants | Dataset |
| Measurement | Evaluation protocol |
| Control group | Baseline models |
| Variable contribution | Ablation study |
| External validity | Generalization claim |

**Important:** Dataset choice, data splits, evaluation metrics, and generalization scope are all parts of the research design.

# 15. Validity Threats

## 15.1 What Is Validity?

Validity refers to the extent to which a research study measures what it intends to measure and supports accurate, justified conclusions.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Validity is the degree to which a research study accurately measures its intended constructs and produces conclusions that are supported by appropriate evidence and analysis.

## 15.2 Types of Validity and Related Risks

### 1. Construct Validity

Question: Are we measuring the concept we claim to measure?

Example: Using only the number of AI interactions to measure learning independence may not accurately represent independent learning ability.

### 2. Internal Validity

Question: Could another factor explain the observed result?

Example: Students' previous programming skills may explain differences in problem-solving performance rather than the AI support type.

### 3. External Validity

Question: Can the findings be generalized beyond the study sample or context?

Example: Findings from one university may not represent all university students.

### 4. Reliability

Question: Would the research process produce consistent results?

Example: Different annotators may assign different labels to the same text.

### 5. Conclusion Validity

Question: Are the research claims supported by the strength and quality of the analysis?

Example: A small sample or inappropriate statistical test may not support a strong conclusion.

### 6. Ethical Validity

Question: Are participants protected, respected, and treated fairly?

Example: Collecting personal data without informed consent creates ethical concerns.

### Summary Table

| Type | Main Concern |
|---|---|
| Construct Validity | Correct measurement |
| Internal Validity | Alternative explanations |
| External Validity | Generalizability |
| Reliability | Consistency |
| Conclusion Validity | Strength of conclusions |
| Ethical Validity | Participant protection |

**Important:** Validity threats should be described specifically. Instead of writing "small sample," write "the sample from one university may not represent working students."

# 16. Research Feasibility

## 16.1 What Is Feasibility?

Feasibility refers to whether a research study can realistically be completed within the available time, resources, skills, data access, and ethical constraints.

**Exam-ready definition:**

Research feasibility is the practical possibility of completing a study considering available resources, time, participant or dataset access, researcher skills, scope, and ethical requirements.

## 16.2 Five Main Feasibility Factors

| Factor | Key Question |
|---|---|
| Access | Can the researcher access participants, datasets, tools, or APIs? |
| Time | Can the study be completed before the deadline? |
| Ethics | Can consent, privacy, and potential risks be managed? |
| Skill | Can the research team correctly perform the required analysis? |
| Scope | Is the research question appropriately limited? |

**Important:** A scientifically attractive study is still a poor research design if it cannot be executed.

# 17. Research Alignment Matrix

## 17.1 What Is an Alignment Matrix?

An alignment matrix is a structured table that connects each research question with its literature gap, evidence source, methodology, analysis technique, and validity risk.

It helps researchers identify whether the study is logically consistent.

### Example Structure

| RQ | Literature Gap | Evidence Source | Method/Analysis | Validity Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RQ1 | Paper Cluster A | Survey items or dataset fields | Descriptive statistics or coding | Recall or label bias |
| RQ2 | Paper Cluster B | Scale, logs, or scenarios | Regression or group comparison | Construct validity |
| RQ3 | Paper Cluster C | Interview or open responses | Thematic analysis | Transferability |

### Evidence/Dataset Status

An additional column should record the availability status:

- Available now
- Likely available
- Needs collection
- Risky
- Not available

### Most Important Rule

**No orphan RQ.**

Every research question must be linked to:
1. A literature gap.
2. An evidence source.
3. A suitable method.
4. A validity risk.

# 18. Research Question Revision Clinic

This classroom activity focuses on improving initial research questions using literature evidence.

### Steps

**Step 1 – Individual (10 minutes)**

Write the current problem statement, initial RQs, and strongest literature gap.

**Step 2 – Rewrite (10 minutes)**

Improve the RQs and remove questions that cannot be answered.

**Step 3 – Peer Review (15 minutes)**

A partner checks:
- Scope
- Evidence path
- Gap link
- Method fit

**Step 4 – Class Share (5 minutes)**

Discuss one strong revised RQ and one weak RQ.

### Main Evaluation Question

"What evidence would convince a skeptical reader that your research question has been answered?"

# 19. Study Design and Evidence Mapping Workshop

The purpose of this workshop is to update the research design outline and group tracker.

### Steps and Outputs

| Step | Task | Expected Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Classify each revised RQ type | Descriptive, relational, causal, interpretive, or design |
| 2 | Map constructs and variables | Operational definitions and evidence sources |
| 3 | Check dataset or participant access | Named sources and quality risks |
| 4 | Choose research design | Survey, experiment, interview, prototype test, or mixed methods |

**Key Question:** Which RQ is most likely to fail because of feasibility, weak evidence, or poor dataset quality?

# 22. Dataset Selection Criteria

Before selecting a dataset, researchers must determine whether it is suitable for the research question.

There are three major categories of dataset fit.

## 22.1 Research Fit

Check whether the dataset:
- Matches the revised research question.
- Uses the correct unit of analysis.
- Represents the required population or context.
- Covers the relevant time period.
- Contains a clear outcome variable.

## 22.2 Technical Fit

Check whether the dataset:
- Contains enough observations for the task.
- Has a usable format and size.
- Has manageable missing values.
- Allows duplicate identification.
- Supports an appropriate train/test split.

## 22.3 Research Quality Fit

Check whether:
- The creator and collection purpose are known.
- The labeling process is documented.
- The license allows research use.
- Bias and coverage can be evaluated.
- Previous baselines are available.

**Important Rule:** A smaller, well-documented dataset with transparent labels is often better than a large dataset with unclear origin, weak labels, or vague licensing.

# 23. Dataset Quality and Fit Evaluation

A dataset should be evaluated using several dimensions.

## 23.1 First Five Dimensions

| Dimension | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Research Fit | Match with RQ, context, population, task, and unit | Forced mapping to a convenient dataset |
| Temporal Fit | Whether the collection period is relevant | Outdated data |
| Variable Fit | Whether required outcome and explanatory variables exist | Missing key variables |
| Granularity Fit | Whether the record-level detail supports the analysis | Only aggregated statistics |
| Provenance | Who collected it, when, why, and how | No source or methodology |

## 23.2 Additional Five Dimensions

| Dimension | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Label Quality | Whether labels are reliable and reproducible | No annotation guide or agreement check |
| Completeness | Missing, noisy, duplicated, or unusable data | More than 20% missing without a plan |
| Representativeness | Whether important groups and regions are covered | One region/language used for a general claim |
| Class Balance | Whether one class dominates | 95/5 imbalance without a mitigation plan |
| Leakage Risk | Whether features accidentally reveal the target | A proxy feature directly reveals the label |
| Ethics and License | Whether the data can legally and ethically be used | No license or identifiable data without proper basis |
| Documentation | Whether variables and preprocessing are explained | Missing or vague README |

**Note:** Values such as 20% missing data or a 95/5 class imbalance are warning examples, not universal rules. Their seriousness depends on the research context and the availability of mitigation strategies.

### Dataset Decision Rule

A well-documented but smaller dataset may be more appropriate than a larger dataset with unclear labels, hidden bias, or uncertain licensing.

Convenience, size, and popularity alone do not establish dataset quality.

# 27. Important Differences for Exams

## 27.1 Research Question vs. Hypothesis

| Research Question | Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Asks what the study investigates | Predicts an expected result |
| Usually written as a question | Written as a statement |
| Guides the research | Guides statistical testing |
| Can be qualitative or quantitative | Mainly used in testable quantitative research |
| Example: Does AI support influence learning independence? | AI-supported students will achieve higher independence scores. |

## 27.2 Construct vs. Variable

| Construct | Variable |
|---|---|
| Abstract concept | Measurable characteristic |
| Not always directly observable | Can be assigned or observed as values |
| Example: Trust | Example: Trust scale score |

## 27.3 Quantitative vs. Qualitative Research

| Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|
| Uses numerical data | Uses non-numerical data |
| Measures variables | Explores meanings and experiences |
| Uses statistical analysis | Uses interpretive analysis |
| Survey and experiment | Interview and observation |
| Often tests hypotheses | Often explores research questions |

## 27.4 Experiment vs. Quasi-Experiment

| Experiment | Quasi-Experiment |
|---|---|
| Researcher manipulates an independent variable | Compares naturally occurring groups or conditions |
| Random assignment is generally used where possible | Random assignment is absent or limited |
| Stronger control over confounding | More vulnerable to confounding |
| Used for causal investigation | Used to estimate effects when full experimentation is not feasible |

## 27.5 Primary vs. Existing Dataset Evidence

| Primary Evidence | Existing Dataset |
|---|---|
| Collected directly for the current study | Previously collected data |
| Survey, interview, observation | Public, institutional, benchmark data |
| Researcher controls the collection process | Researcher depends on existing collection methods |
| Requires a collection plan | Requires provenance and quality assessment |

## 27.6 Research Design vs. Research Method

| Research Design | Research Method |
|---|---|
| Overall study plan | Specific data collection or analysis technique |
| Connects RQs, evidence, and analysis | Executes a particular research activity |
| Example: Mixed-methods design | Example: Survey and interview |

# 28. Important Short Questions and Exam-Ready Answers

## Q1. What is a research question?

A research question is a focused and researchable question that identifies the specific issue a study aims to investigate. It guides the selection of evidence, methodology, and analysis.

## Q2. Why is research question revision necessary?

Research question revision is necessary because initial questions are often too broad, unclear, or unsupported by evidence. Literature review helps identify research gaps and refine the scope, population, variables, and methodology.

## Q3. What is a research gap?

A research gap is an unanswered or insufficiently investigated issue in existing literature that provides a justification for conducting a new study.

## Q4. What is a hypothesis?

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about an expected relationship or difference between variables based on existing theory or previous research.

## Q5. What is the difference between H0 and H1?

H0 (null hypothesis) states that there is no significant relationship or difference between variables. H1 (alternative hypothesis) states that a significant relationship or difference is expected.

## Q6. What is operationalization?

Operationalization is the process of translating abstract research constructs into specific observable indicators or measurable variables.

## Q7. Why is operationalization important?

Operationalization is important because it makes abstract concepts measurable or observable, allowing researchers to collect evidence and systematically analyze research questions.

## Q8. What is research design?

Research design is the overall plan that specifies how a study will collect, measure, and analyze evidence to answer its research questions.

## Q9. What are the main types of research questions?

The main types are descriptive, comparative, relational, causal, interpretive, and design/evaluation research questions.

## Q10. What is mixed-methods research?

Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide a more comprehensive understanding of a research problem.

## Q11. Name three types of mixed-methods design.

1. Sequential explanatory (QUAN → qual).
2. Sequential exploratory (qual → QUAN).
3. Convergent (QUAN + qual).

## Q12. What is validity?

Validity is the degree to which a research study accurately measures its intended constructs and supports justified conclusions.

## Q13. What are the six validity considerations discussed in Week 4?

1. Construct validity
2. Internal validity
3. External validity
4. Reliability
5. Conclusion validity
6. Ethical validity

## Q14. What is research feasibility?

Research feasibility refers to whether a study can realistically be completed within the available time, resources, skills, data access, and ethical constraints.

## Q15. What is an alignment matrix?

An alignment matrix is a structured table that connects research questions with literature gaps, evidence sources, methods, analysis techniques, and validity risks.

## Q16. Why is dataset provenance important?

Dataset provenance identifies who collected the data, when and why it was collected, how it was collected, and how it was processed. It helps researchers evaluate data credibility, quality, and limitations.

## Q17. Why is dataset documentation important?

Dataset documentation explains the dataset's structure, variables, collection process, preprocessing, limitations, and permitted use. It supports reproducibility and responsible research.

## Q18. What is data leakage?

Data leakage occurs when information that would not legitimately be available during prediction is included in model training or evaluation, resulting in overly optimistic performance estimates.

## Q19. Why should researchers avoid selecting datasets based only on popularity?

A popular dataset may not match the research question, population, context, or required variables. It may also contain bias, weak labels, missing documentation, or licensing problems.

## Q20. What is the meaning of "No orphan RQ"?

It means every research question must be connected to a specific literature gap, evidence source, suitable method, and validity risk. No research question should remain unsupported by the study design.

# 29. Important Broad Questions and Model Answers

## Q1. Explain how an initial research idea is transformed into a revised study design.

**Answer:**

An initial research idea is transformed into a revised study design through a systematic process of literature-based revision.

First, the researcher prepares an initial problem statement and research questions. Next, a literature review is conducted to identify existing knowledge and research gaps. The initial problem statement is then revised to define a specific problem, context, population, and gap.

Research questions are refined to make them clear, focused, and answerable. Each question is connected to a literature gap and an appropriate evidence source. The researcher then identifies relevant constructs, variables, and operational definitions.

Finally, an appropriate research design, data source, methodology, analysis technique, validity considerations, and feasibility plan are selected.

Thus, the revised study design becomes a literature-aligned and executable research plan.

## Q2. Explain the characteristics of a strong research question.

**Answer:**

A strong research question should be clear, focused, researchable, and supported by the literature.

Its main characteristics include:

1. **Gap Link:** It addresses an identifiable gap in existing literature.
2. **Evidence:** It specifies what type of evidence can answer the question.
3. **Method Fit:** Its research method is appropriate for the question type.
4. **Scope:** It clearly defines the population, context, dataset, or artifact.
5. **Construct Clarity:** Its important concepts are measurable or interpretable.
6. **Feasibility:** It can be completed within the available time and resources.

A strong RQ connects an unresolved issue in the literature with a realistic evidence collection and analysis plan.

## Q3. Explain different types of research questions with suitable methods.

**Answer:**

Research questions can be classified into six major types.

1. **Descriptive:** Investigates what is happening. Suitable methods include surveys, logs, and content analysis.
2. **Comparative:** Examines differences between groups. Suitable methods include experiments, surveys, and statistical tests.
3. **Relational:** Investigates associations between variables. Suitable methods include correlation, regression, and SEM.
4. **Causal:** Examines whether one variable causes a change in another. Suitable methods include experiments and quasi-experiments.
5. **Interpretive:** Explores human experiences and meanings. Suitable methods include interviews, observations, and thematic analysis.
6. **Design/Evaluation:** Evaluates whether an artifact or system achieves its intended purpose. Suitable methods include prototype development, usability testing, and field studies.

The research question should determine the appropriate methodology.

## Q4. Explain hypotheses and their components with an example.

**Answer:**

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about an expected relationship or difference between variables.

A good hypothesis contains four components:

1. Independent Variable (IV): The factor that changes or differs.
2. Dependent Variable (DV): The outcome being measured.
3. Direction: The expected increase, decrease, or relationship.
4. Comparison: The conditions or groups being compared.

Example:

Research Question: Does AI support type influence independent problem-solving performance?

H1: Students using step-by-step AI scaffolds will achieve higher independent problem-solving scores than students using answer-only AI support.

H0: There is no difference in independent problem-solving scores between the two groups.

Hypotheses are appropriate when previous research supports a testable expectation and statistical analysis is planned. They are not mandatory for every exploratory or qualitative study.

## Q5. Explain operationalization with an example.

**Answer:**

Operationalization is the process of translating abstract research constructs into observable indicators and measurable variables.

For example, trust is an abstract construct that cannot always be observed directly. Researchers may define its dimensions as credibility, reliability, and confidence. These dimensions can be measured using questionnaire items on a five-point Likert scale.

The resulting trust score can then be analyzed using descriptive statistics, regression, or group comparison.

The process is:

**Construct → Dimensions → Indicators → Measures → Analysis**

Operationalization makes abstract concepts suitable for systematic research.

## Q6. Compare quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research.

**Answer:**

Quantitative research uses numerical data to measure variables, examine relationships, and test hypotheses. Common methods include surveys, experiments, and statistical analysis.

Qualitative research uses non-numerical data to explore human experiences, perceptions, and meanings. Common methods include interviews, observations, focus groups, and thematic analysis.

Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide a more comprehensive understanding of a research problem.

The appropriate approach depends on the research questions and the types of evidence needed.

## Q7. Explain the different validity threats in research.

**Answer:**

Validity threats are factors that may weaken the accuracy, reliability, or generalizability of research findings.

The major considerations are:

1. **Construct Validity:** Whether the study measures the intended concept.
2. **Internal Validity:** Whether alternative factors could explain the result.
3. **External Validity:** Whether findings can be generalized beyond the study context.
4. **Reliability:** Whether the research process produces consistent results.
5. **Conclusion Validity:** Whether the conclusions are supported by appropriate analysis.
6. **Ethical Validity:** Whether participants are protected and treated fairly.

Researchers should identify potential threats during study design and plan suitable mitigation strategies.

## Q8. Explain the criteria for selecting a suitable dataset.

**Answer:**

A suitable dataset should be evaluated using three major categories.

**1. Research Fit**
- Matches the research question.
- Represents the correct population and context.
- Contains relevant variables.
- Covers the required time period.

**2. Technical Fit**
- Contains sufficient observations.
- Has a usable format and size.
- Has manageable missing values and duplicates.
- Supports an appropriate train/test split.

**3. Research Quality Fit**
- Has clear provenance.
- Uses documented labeling procedures.
- Has an appropriate license.
- Has identifiable limitations and biases.
- Provides sufficient documentation.

A smaller, well-documented dataset may be preferable to a larger dataset with unclear origin or poor label quality.

## Q9. Explain the importance of an alignment matrix.

**Answer:**

An alignment matrix ensures consistency between the research questions, literature gaps, evidence sources, methodology, and validity considerations.

It helps researchers:
- Identify unsupported research questions.
- Confirm that appropriate evidence is available.
- Select suitable research methods.
- Identify potential validity threats.
- Evaluate the feasibility of the study.

Each research question should have a clear literature gap, evidence source, method, analysis plan, and validity risk.

## Q10. Explain the major components of an ML or data science research design.

**Answer:**

An ML or data science research design consists of four major components.

1. **Task Framing:** Define the prediction, classification, detection, or generation task precisely.
2. **Dataset Selection:** Select and document a dataset that matches the research question and target population.
3. **Evaluation Protocol:** Choose suitable metrics, baseline models, data splits, and ablation studies.
4. **Generalization Claim:** Define the population, domain, or conditions to which the model's findings are expected to apply.

Dataset selection and evaluation are research design decisions because they determine what the model can learn and how its performance can be justified.
